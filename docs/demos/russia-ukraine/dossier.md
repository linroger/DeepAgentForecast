# The Russia-Ukraine War in June 2026: A Comprehensive Strategic Assessment

**By DeerFlow Research | Updated 9 June 2026**

Four years, three months, and two weeks after Russian forces crossed the Ukrainian border on 24 February 2022, the war in Ukraine has entered a new and more ambiguous phase. It is no longer a story of dramatic offensives and rapid territorial shifts, nor is it the frozen "forever war" that some analysts had predicted. Instead, it has settled into a grinding attritional equilibrium on the battlefield, an intensifying diplomatic sprint, a brittle sanctions regime under fresh strain, and an emerging political crisis inside Ukraine itself that may prove as consequential as anything happening on the front lines. This assessment synthesises the most recent authoritative data from the Institute for the Study of War (ISW), the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), the European Commission, UN agencies, and major international media to map where the war stands, why it is where it is, and what comes next.

## 1. The Strategic Picture: Stalemate by Attrition

### 1.1 Territorial control

As of May 2026, Russian forces occupy approximately 20% of Ukraine's internationally recognised territory, including Crimea (annexed in 2014) and large parts of the four oblasts Moscow claimed to have annexed in September 2022 — Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson [citation:Russo-Ukrainian war (2022–present) – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)). The Ukrainian-held portion of the Donbas — most notably the city of Pokrovsk and its twin city Myrnohrad — fell to Russian forces in early 2026 after a multi-year siege that became symbolic of the war's grinding character [citation:Pokrovsk offensive – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pokrovsk_offensive). Pokrovsk had been a major road and rail junction and a key logistics node; its loss shifts the Russian operational focus toward the Kramatorsk–Kostiantynivka urban agglomeration, the last significant Ukrainian redoubt in Donetsk Oblast [citation:Why the fall of Pokrovsk would matter to Ukraine and Russia – BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c0jdv0623pno).

The capture came at a cost that few in Moscow have been willing to publicly acknowledge. Pokrovsk was described by Ukrainian and Western analysts as having been reduced to rubble during the assault, with Russian units forced to clear the city block by block over many months [citation:Ukraine faces potential fall of Pokrovsk to Russia after 18 months of fighting – ABC News](https://abcnews.com/International/ukraine-faces-potential-fall-pokrovsk-russia-after-18/story?id=127160913).

### 1.2 The pace of Russian advance has collapsed

Perhaps the single most under-reported development of 2026 is how dramatically the Russian advance has slowed. According to the ISW assessment published on 1 June 2026, Russian forces gained a presence in only 40.64 square kilometres between December 2025 and May 2026, including not just confirmed territorial gains but also "infiltrations" by small groups operating behind Ukrainian lines [citation:Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, June 1, 2026 – ISW](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-june-1-2026). In the equivalent period a year earlier, Russian forces seized 515.84 square kilometres. That means the Russian army in spring 2026 is advancing or infiltrating into only 7.87% of the territory it captured in spring 2025 — a more than tenfold reduction in operational tempo [citation:ISW, June 1, 2026](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-june-1-2026).

When only territory under confirmed Russian control is counted, the picture is even starker. In early 2025, Russia was gaining roughly 100 square kilometres per month. By early 2026, after a Ukrainian counter-offensive, the net Russian controlled-territory change was a loss of approximately 281 square kilometres between December 2025 and May 2026. The March 2026 ISW assessment, titled "Ukraine Seizes the Initiative," noted that for the first time since the 2024 Kursk operation, Ukrainian forces had reclaimed more territory in a single month than Russia had captured [citation:Ukraine Seizes the Initiative – ISW, March 2026](https://www.understandingwar.org/research/russia-ukraine/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-march-31-2026/).

This does not mean Russia is losing the war. It means the war has entered a phase in which the front line is essentially static, with both sides trading small salients at unsustainable cost. The character of combat has changed accordingly — high in casualty generation, low in territorial yield — and this is the dynamic that defines the strategic environment in which the diplomatic push of 2026 is now taking place.

### 1.3 The human cost

The casualty figures from this phase are staggering. According to a CSIS study published in January 2026, the combined Russian and Ukrainian military casualty count — killed, wounded, and missing — approached 2 million by late 2025, and was projected to reach that figure by spring 2026 [citation:Ukraine war briefing: Nearly 2 million military casualties to date, study finds – The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jan/28/ukraine-war-briefing-nearly-2-million-military-casualties-to-date-study).

Russia has borne the brunt of the losses. CSIS estimates that of approximately 1.2 million Russian casualties since February 2022, as many as 325,000 have been deaths [citation:Russian and Ukrainian military losses approach 2 million, study reveals – Walaw](https://en.walaw.press/articles/russian_and_ukrainian_military_losses_approach_2_million_study_reveals/GQQLQSXSMLQX). Independent Russian-language outlets, the BBC's Russian service and Mediazona, have confirmed more than 163,000 Russian military deaths through public records, while noting that the true figure is necessarily higher [citation:The Guardian, January 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jan/28/ukraine-war-briefing-nearly-2-million-military-casualties-to-date-study).

Ukraine's losses, while proportionally smaller, are catastrophic for a country of its pre-war population. CSIS estimates 500,000 to 600,000 Ukrainian military casualties, of which between 100,000 and 140,000 are deaths. President Zelensky's February 2025 figure of "nearly 46,000" Ukrainian dead is widely viewed as an underestimate [citation:The Guardian, January 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jan/28/ukraine-war-briefing-nearly-2-million-military-casualties-to-date-study).

Civilian casualties have continued to mount. According to the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, nearly 15,000 Ukrainian civilians have been verified killed and more than 40,600 injured since February 2022, with the actual totals believed to be considerably higher [citation:Russia-Ukraine War – Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russia-Ukraine-War). Russian strikes on urban energy and transport infrastructure in the winter of 2025–2026 produced acute mass-casualty events, including a January 2026 drone strike on a passenger train in Kharkiv Oblast that killed five people, and a wave of attacks that left 710,000 residents of Kyiv without electricity and central heating in sub-zero temperatures [citation:The Guardian, January 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jan/28/ukraine-war-briefing-nearly-2-million-military-casualties-to-date-study).

## 2. The Diplomatic Sprint: From the 28-Point Plan to Abu Dhabi

### 2.1 The Trump 28-point plan

The most consequential diplomatic event of the war's fourth year has been the unveiling, in late 2025, of a 28-point peace plan drafted by the Trump administration's special envoy Steve Witkoff and Secretary of State Marco Rubio [citation:Trump Approves 28-Point Russia-Ukraine Peace Plan – Mezha](https://mezha.net/eng/bukvy/trump-approves-28-point-russia-ukraine-peace-plan-amid-ongoing-talks/). The plan was developed in close coordination with Russian envoy Kirill Dmitriev and incorporates feedback from both Russian and Ukrainian officials.

The core provisions are severe. According to the publicly reported text, the plan requires Ukraine to cede the entirety of Crimea, Luhansk, and Donetsk to Russia — including the roughly one-third of Donetsk Oblast that Ukrainian forces still held at the time the plan was drafted — and to freeze the front line in Kherson and Zaporizhzhia along existing contact lines [citation:Trump's 28-point Ukraine peace plan in full – Sky News](https://news.sky.com/story/trumps-28-point-ukraine-peace-plan-in-full-including-land-kyiv-must-hand-to-russia-and-when-elections-must-be-held-13473491). The plan would also cap the size of the Ukrainian Armed Forces, restrict Ukraine's access to long-range strike weapons, and commit Kyiv to holding elections within a specified timeframe.

In exchange, Ukraine would receive formal U.S. and international security guarantees short of NATO membership, approximately $100 billion in frozen Russian sovereign assets for reconstruction, and a path toward eventual EU accession [citation:Trump's Ukraine Proposal Includes Land Concessions, NATO Limits – Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/trump-russia-ukraine-war-peace-plan-draft-land-nato-g8-11084995).

### 2.2 Ukrainian objections

President Zelensky has been categorical that Ukraine cannot legally or politically accept the territorial terms as initially proposed. After meeting Trump in Florida in late 2025, Zelensky said the land issue remained unresolved [citation:Trump after meeting with Zelensky: territorial issues unresolved – News-Pravda](https://news-pravda.com/world/2025/12/29/1962395.html). He has publicly argued that ceding Donbas would create a launchpad for future Russian aggression, and that the relevant Ukrainian constitutional and legal provisions prohibit unilateral territorial concessions [citation:Zelensky Rules Out Territorial Concessions – Novinite](https://www.novinite.com/articles/236175/Zelensky+Rules+Out+Territorial+Concessions,+Says+Ukraine+Cannot+Simply+Withdraw+from+Donbas).

Kyiv has offered a counter-proposal: a free trade zone under Ukrainian sovereignty in the still-contested parts of eastern Ukraine, with the question of final political status deferred [citation:Ukraine, Russia, U.S. to discuss fraught issue of territorial concessions – Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/world-nation/story/2026-01-23/russia-ukraine-u-s-are-holding-peace-talks-in-abu-dhabi). The proposal is creative but has not been formally accepted by Moscow, which continues to insist on full Ukrainian withdrawal from the remainder of Donetsk as a non-negotiable condition.

### 2.3 The Abu Dhabi talks and European pressure

In late January 2026, the United States, Ukraine, and Russia convened three-way talks in Abu Dhabi, the first known occasion on which Trump-administration envoys (Steve Witkoff, Jared Kushner, Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, and NATO's Supreme Allied Commander Europe, General Alexus Grynkewich) met simultaneously with delegations from both Kyiv and Moscow [citation:Los Angeles Times, January 2026](https://www.latimes.com/world-nation/story/2026-01-23/russia-ukraine-u-s-are-holding-peace-talks-in-abu-dhabi). The Russian delegation was headed by Admiral Igor Kostyukov, head of the GRU military intelligence service, signalling Moscow's elevation of the military channel of the negotiation.

The European Union has been working to influence the process from the outside. The EU joined the Geneva talks, with European negotiators pushing back on what they view as the most punitive elements of the U.S. draft, particularly the limits on Ukrainian force size and the implicit acceptance of Russian sovereignty over seized territory [citation:EU to join Geneva talks on Ukraine, will push Trump team – RBC Ukraine](https://newsukraine.rbc.ua/news/eu-to-join-geneva-talks-on-ukraine-will-push-1763890941.html). Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has publicly argued that any extension of NATO's Article 5 security guarantee to Ukraine would be a meaningful test of Russia's intentions [citation:Extending NATO's Article 5 to Ukraine would test Russia's peace intentions – Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/extending-natos-article-5-to-ukraine-would-test-russias-peace-intentions-meloni-says/).

The fundamental impasse remains the same as it has been for three years: Kyiv will not formally recognise Russian sovereignty over any of the five claimed oblasts, and Moscow will not agree to a settlement that leaves any of those territories under Ukrainian control. The difference in 2026 is that the United States has shifted from rhetorical support for Ukraine to active mediation, and that the economic cost of continued war is starting to bend Moscow's posture — but not yet enough.

## 3. The Economic Dimension: Strain on Both Sides

### 3.1 Russia: From boom to "managed cooling"

The story of Russia's wartime economy in 2026 is one of slowdown approaching stagnation. After posting GDP growth of 4.1% in 2023 and 4.9% in 2024 — figures that surprised most Western analysts who had predicted a sanctions-induced collapse — the Russian economy has decelerated sharply [citation:Cracks in Russia's Economy Shine Through at SPIEF – Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/06/05/cracks-in-russias-economy-shine-through-at-spief-a92943). Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak announced in May 2026 that the official 2026 GDP growth forecast had been cut from 1.3% to 0.4% [citation:Cracks in Russia's Economy Shine Through at SPIEF – Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/06/05/cracks-in-russias-economy-shine-through-at-spief-a92943). Some Russian central bank officials have even warned of a possible recession by year-end [citation:Moscow Times, June 2026](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/06/05/cracks-in-russias-economy-shine-through-at-spief-a92943).

The structural drivers of the slowdown are familiar but intensifying. Defence spending has crowded out civilian investment; the labour market is tight due to mobilisation and emigration; oil and gas revenues, the traditional backbone of the budget, are constrained by both sanctions and Ukrainian drone attacks on refining infrastructure. At the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum in early June 2026, the central narrative was a quiet admission that the wartime economy is the permanent economy, in the words of Kremlin official Maxim Oreshkin: "We shouldn't expect things to return to the way they were, or for Western sanctions to be lifted" [citation:Cracks in Russia's Economy Shine Through at SPIEF – Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/06/05/cracks-in-russias-economy-shine-through-at-spief-a92943).

### 3.2 Sanctions: The 20th package and the shadow fleet

On 23 April 2026, the EU formally adopted its 20th sanctions package against Russia, characterised by the European Commission as having a "strong anti-circumvention angle" [citation:EU adopts 20th package of sanctions against Russia – European Commission](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/news/eu-adopts-20th-package-sanctions-against-russia-2026-04-23_en). The package includes 36 new listings in the Russian energy sector covering exploration, extraction, refining, and transportation; the listing of an additional 46 vessels in Russia's "shadow fleet," bringing the total to 632 listed vessels subject to port access bans and service prohibitions; the listing of two Russian ports (Murmansk and Tuapse) and, for the first time, a third-country port (Karimun Oil Terminal in Indonesia) for shadow-fleet-related activity; and the legal basis for a future prohibition on the transport of Russian crude oil and petroleum products [citation:EU 20th package, Kinstellar](https://www.kinstellar.com/news-and-insights/detail/4429/eu-20th-sanctions-package-against-russia).

The package also extended the transaction ban to an additional 20 Russian banks — bringing the total to 70 Russian banks excluded from the EU internal market — and to four banks in third countries (Kyrgyzstan, Laos, Azerbaijan) considered to be enabling sanctions evasion [citation:European Commission, April 23, 2026](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/news/eu-adopts-20th-package-sanctions-against-russia-2026-04-23_en).

For the first time, the EU activated its "anti-circumvention" tool, which is designed to crack down on third-country entities that materially support Russian sanctions evasion. The package is also working in tandem with the G7 price cap coalition to prepare a future maritime services ban on Russian crude oil and petroleum products [citation:European Commission, April 23, 2026](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/news/eu-adopts-20th-package-sanctions-against-russia-2026-04-23_en).

There are, however, visible cracks. The UK government in May 2026 quietly issued a licence allowing imports of Russian oil refined in third countries, a relaxation criticised by opposition leader Kemi Badenoch as a back-door concession [citation:UK delays some sanctions on Russian oil and gas – The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/may/20/uk-relaxes-strict-sanctions-on-russian-crude-oil). The United States has also temporarily relaxed enforcement of certain sanctions on Russian oil as part of a broader effort to stabilise global energy markets. The BOFIT weekly review noted in June 2026 that "Russian oil exports have also benefited recently by the temporary relaxation of sanctions on Russian oil by both the US and the UK" [citation:Russian economy stabilising after weak start of the year – BOFIT](https://www.bofit.fi/en/monitoring/weekly/2026/vw202623_1/).

### 3.3 Ukraine: Demographic crisis and external dependence

The Ukrainian economy in 2026 remains entirely dependent on external support to maintain basic state functions. Ukraine's pre-war population of approximately 41 million has shrunk to roughly 28 million due to war, displacement, and emigration [citation:The war is rapidly shrinking Ukraine's population – European Pravda](https://unn.ua/en/news/the-war-is-rapidly-shrinking-ukraines-population-and-even-eu-accession-will-not-stop-the-demographic-crisis-euobserver). Of those who have left, 5.6 million are recorded as refugees abroad as of early 2026, and approximately 8 million are internally displaced persons [citation:Russo-Ukrainian war (2022–present) – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)) [citation:UNHCR Ukraine Refugee Situation](https://data.unhcr.org/en/situations/ukraine). The economic implications are severe: a shrinking working-age population, a hollowed-out tax base, and a defence burden that consumes a majority of the state budget.

Ukraine's external support requirements continue to be substantial. According to the Kiel Institute's Ukraine Support Tracker, which tracks aid allocations through April 2026, the United States remains the single largest bilateral donor, having committed roughly $120 billion in total support since 2022, including military, humanitarian, and financial aid [citation:Ukraine weapons: What arms are the US, UK and other nations – BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-62002218). European Union institutions and member states have contributed roughly 18.3% of total support [citation:BBC Ukraine weapons](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-62002218).

The Trump administration has signalled a willingness to continue some level of support, but the 2027 U.S. federal budget submitted to Congress omits funding for the Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative (USAI) — the principal vehicle for U.S. military aid — even as the 2026 tranche of $400 million remained in slow distribution as of early 2026 [citation:U.S. 2027 budget omits funding for Ukraine military aid program – New Voice of Ukraine](https://english.nv.ua/nation/u-s-2027-budget-omits-funding-for-ukraine-military-aid-program-50604819.html). The Kiel Institute's analysis identifies a "widening gap between military and non-military aid," with European military contributions increasingly carrying the load [citation:Ukraine Support Tracker – Kiel Institute](https://www.kielinstitut.de/topics/war-against-ukraine/ukraine-support-tracker/).

## 4. The Military Balance: A War of Drones and Dwindling Stocks

### 4.1 Manpower

The manpower comparison between the two sides has shifted in interesting ways. Russia, after conducting a partial mobilisation in September 2022 and sustained covert conscription drives since, fields an active force in Ukraine estimated at over 700,000 personnel as of mid-2024 [citation:Russo-Ukrainian war – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)). Ukraine, with a much smaller pre-war mobilisation base, has been forced to adopt increasingly desperate measures to maintain force levels, with the average soldier's age rising and frontline units often under-strength.

The casualty dynamics — Russia losing approximately 1.2 million total and Ukraine approximately 500,000–600,000 — are not sustainable indefinitely for either side, but the political tolerance for absorbing losses is clearly higher in Moscow than in Kyiv. Russia has been willing to write off the demographic and economic cost of mobilisation, treating it as a national-security cost. Ukraine, by contrast, faces a population crisis that constrains its ability to generate forces without hollowing out its economy and its future.

### 4.2 Equipment and ammunition

The ammunition question has been central. Ukraine's dependence on Western 155mm artillery ammunition peaked in 2023–2024 and has since been partially alleviated by European production scaling, though not eliminated. Russia's domestic production of artillery shells, glide bombs, and (increasingly) Shahed-type one-way attack drones has outpaced Ukraine's air defence capacity to intercept them.

Russian nightly drone and missile barrages have become a defining feature of the war. According to daily reporting from the Ukrainian Air Force, Russia has been launching strike packages of 200–300 drones in a single overnight wave, alongside varying numbers of cruise and ballistic missiles. The cumulative effect on Ukrainian energy infrastructure has been severe: by January 2026, 45% of schools in Kyiv were closed due to a lack of central heating, and the Ukrainian government was building a second level of physical protection at 120 critical energy infrastructure sites [citation:Ukraine Builds Second Level Protection on 120 Energy Infrastructure Sites – Mezha](https://mezha.net/eng/bukvy/ukraine-builds-second-level-protection-on-120-energy-infrastructure-sites-amid-russian-attacks/) [citation:'Everything is frozen': bitter winter drags on for Kyiv residents – The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/feb/13/everything-is-frozen-bitter-winter-drags-on-for-kyiv-residents-as-russia-wipes-out-power).

### 4.3 The drone war and Starlink

The 2026 conflict is in many respects a drone war. Ukraine pioneered the use of small commercial and improvised first-person-view drones for reconnaissance and strike missions, and has now fielded long-range strike drones capable of attacking Russian refining and military infrastructure deep inside Russia — including, as noted above, the drone strike on St. Petersburg during the economic forum in early June 2026 [citation:Moscow Times, June 2026](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/06/05/cracks-in-russias-economy-shine-through-at-spief-a92943).

Russia, in turn, has been forced to use the Starlink satellite internet service to guide its own drone operations, a fact confirmed by the Institute for the Study of War and publicly criticised by Poland's foreign minister Radosław Sikorski, who wrote on X: "Hey, big man, @elonmusk, why don't you stop the Russians from using Starlinks to target Ukrainian cities. Making money on war crimes may damage your brand" [citation:The Guardian, January 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jan/28/ukraine-war-briefing-nearly-2-million-military-casualties-to-date-study).

## 5. Inside Ukraine: The Corruption Crisis and the Question of Legitimacy

### 5.1 Operation Midas and the Yermak affair

The most consequential political development inside Ukraine in late 2025 and early 2026 has been "Operation Midas," an anti-corruption investigation conducted by the National Anti-Corruption Bureau of Ukraine (NABU) and the Specialized Anti-Corruption Prosecutor's Office (SAPO) into major graft in the energy sector [citation:Operation Midas – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Midas). The investigation has directly implicated figures close to President Zelensky, including former Energy Minister and Deputy Prime Minister Herman Halushchenko and former Justice Minister Svitlana Grynchuk, both of whom resigned in November 2025 under pressure from the president [citation:Major corruption scandal engulfs top Zelensky allies – BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cy8vw62j3g9o).

The scandal deepened in May 2026 when NABU and SAPO brought formal charges against Andriy Yermak, Zelensky's former chief of staff and one of the most powerful figures in the president's inner circle, in connection with kickback allegations involving the state nuclear operator Energoatom [citation:Zelensky's Former Right-Hand Man Andriy Yermak Charged – Gateway Pundit](https://www.thegatewaypundit.com/2026/05/just-zelenskys-former-right-hand-man-andriy-yermak/).

The political fallout has been significant. The corruption allegations, which surfaced at a moment of acute fiscal stress, have weakened Zelensky's negotiating position, complicated the diplomatic track, and energised domestic critics who argue that wartime mobilisation and reconstruction have provided cover for large-scale graft.

### 5.2 Elections and the legitimacy question

The Trump administration's 28-point peace plan, and public statements from the White House, have included a Ukrainian election requirement. The Washington deadline reportedly calls for presidential elections in May [citation:Ukraine elections 2026 – RBC Ukraine](https://newsukraine.rbc.ua/analytics/zelenskyy-s-tough-call-why-white-house-urging-1770886633.html). Under Ukrainian law, presidential elections are constitutionally prohibited during martial law, which has been continuously in effect since February 2022 and was extended in February 2025 by a unanimous Verkhovna Rada resolution affirming Zelensky's mandate until the end of martial law [citation:Ukrainian parliament extends Zelensky's mandate – Novaya Gazeta Europe](https://novayagazeta.eu/articles/2025/02/25/ukrainian-parliament-extends-zelenskys-mandate-as-president-until-end-of-martial-law-en-news) [citation:Ukrainian parliament affirms Zelenskyy's legitimacy – Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/25/ukrainian-parliament-affirms-zelenskyys-legitimacy).

Western analysts, including at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs (SWP), have noted that the suspension of elections under martial law is a common democratic practice and that Zelensky's mandate remains constitutionally clear [citation:Ukraine: Zelenskyi's Legitimacy Undisputed – ZOiS Berlin](https://www.zois-berlin.de/en/publications/zois-spotlight/ukraine-zelenskyis-legitimacy-undisputed). However, the question of whether and when elections can be safely held has now become a tangible element of the diplomatic negotiation, and one that Kyiv and Washington have not yet fully resolved.

## 6. The Humanitarian Situation: A Slow-Motion Catastrophe

The war has produced one of the largest forced displacement crises in the world. According to UNHCR, more than 5.6 million Ukrainians are recorded as refugees in Europe and Asia as of early 2026, and approximately 8 million are internally displaced [citation:UNHCR Ukraine Refugee Situation](https://data.unhcr.org/en/situations/ukraine) [citation:Russo-Ukrainian war – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)). Ukraine's population of approximately 28 million is the lowest in its modern history as an independent state.

The civilian infrastructure toll is severe. Repeated Russian strikes on the energy grid have left hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians without electricity, heating, and water during the winter months. UNICEF and the WHO have warned that millions of lives are at risk in sub-zero temperatures when heating infrastructure is destroyed. The January 2026 attack wave left 710,000 Kyiv residents without electricity and heating; in the following weeks, 45% of Kyiv's schools were closed [citation:UNHCR Ukraine Refugee Situation](https://data.unhcr.org/en/situations/ukraine) [citation:Millions of lives under threat in Ukraine this winter – BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-63708223) [citation:Guardian, February 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/feb/13/everything-is-frozen-bitter-winter-drags-on-for-kyiv-residents-as-russia-wipes-out-power).

The war has also produced a profound mental health crisis. The WHO has described the conflict as producing one of the largest populations of people suffering from combat-related trauma in modern history, with consequences that will outlast any ceasefire by decades.

## 7. Scenarios: What Comes Next?

Four broad trajectories can be identified for the remainder of 2026 and into 2027.

### 7.1 Negotiated settlement (probability: moderate)

The most likely path, if a settlement is reached, is a phased agreement: a ceasefire along current contact lines, partial international recognition of Russian control over Crimea and existing occupied territory, Ukrainian security guarantees short of NATO membership, a cap on Ukrainian force size, and a multi-year reconstruction package funded partly by frozen Russian sovereign assets. The 28-point plan as initially drafted is the rough template; in practice, expect substantial modifications on force size, NATO-language, and the timing of elections. The obstacles are severe — Kyiv cannot accept ceding Donetsk wholesale, Moscow cannot accept a settlement that does not — but the political will on both sides for some form of deal appears to be higher than at any point since 2022.

### 7.2 Protracted war (probability: significant)

A second scenario is that the diplomatic track fails, and the war continues into 2027 with the current attritional character. Under this scenario, Russian operational tempo remains low, Ukrainian counter-offensives remain local and limited, and the front line remains essentially static. The economic costs on both sides would continue to mount. This scenario is favoured by the structural facts on the ground: the front line is too long for either side to break through decisively, and both sides are too economically strained to sustain a major strategic offensive.

### 7.3 Russian economic collapse (probability: low but rising)

A third scenario is that the cumulative weight of sanctions, falling oil and gas revenues, mobilisation-driven labour shortages, and a possible EU maritime services ban push the Russian economy into a sharp recession. Under this scenario, the political pressure on Putin to seek a deal intensifies, and the diplomatic terms shift substantially in Ukraine's favour. The 20th EU sanctions package, the activation of the anti-circumvention tool, and the potential future maritime services ban on Russian oil are all designed to make this scenario more likely.

### 7.4 Escalation (probability: low but non-zero)

A fourth scenario involves escalation — direct NATO involvement following a major Russian strike on a NATO member, the use of a tactical nuclear weapon in Ukraine, or a Russian attack on a Western logistics hub — that would fundamentally change the character of the war. The presence of senior NATO military officials, including NATO's Supreme Allied Commander Europe, at the Abu Dhabi talks is itself a signal that the alliance is preparing for a range of contingencies.

## 8. Conclusion: A War That Will Not End Quickly, and Will Not End Well

The Russia-Ukraine war in June 2026 is in a paradox. The battlefield is largely static, yet the political and economic dynamics are in rapid motion. The casualty toll has reached levels not seen in Europe since 1945, yet the diplomatic momentum is the highest it has been in three years. Russia's economy is in slow-motion decline, yet the regime's hold on power appears secure for the foreseeable future. Ukraine is fighting for its survival, yet it is also fighting a war of national identity and historical memory that no peace deal, however favourable, can fully resolve.

The most important single fact about the war in June 2026 is this: the territorial front line is no longer moving meaningfully in either direction. The Russian spring-summer 2026 offensive has been "largely halted" by Ukrainian forces, according to ISW [citation:ISW, June 1, 2026](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-june-1-2026). The war is being decided in conference rooms in Geneva, Abu Dhabi, and Davos, in sanctions committees in Brussels, in the corridors of the Verkhovna Rada, and in the polling booths that may or may not open. It is being decided by whether Europe can sustain military aid to Ukraine without U.S. leadership, whether the U.S. can hold Russia to any commitments it makes, and whether the Ukrainian people can be persuaded to accept a peace that falls short of the full restoration of their territorial integrity.

The war is not over. It may not be over for a long time. But it has entered a phase in which its outcome will be determined less by the trajectory of bullets and shells and more by the trajectory of politics, economics, and diplomacy. The question is no longer who is winning on the battlefield. The question is who can outlast the other — in blood, in treasure, and in political will.
