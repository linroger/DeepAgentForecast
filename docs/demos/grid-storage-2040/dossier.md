## Executive Summary

The 2026–2040 grid-scale storage build-out will be governed less by chemistry cost curves than by the duration mix that regulators and system operators are willing to pay for. The dossier's load-bearing thesis is that lithium iron phosphate (LFP) will retain the four-hour niche through 2030 on the strength of a ~90% real cost collapse since 2010, a US$140/kWh cell floor, and a Chinese integrator base that controls roughly 76% of the 2025 global BESS market and roughly 85% of cell-manufacturing capacity, but that the four-to-twelve-hour and multi-day segments will progressively migrate to sodium-ion, flow batteries, thermal, compressed-air, and hydrogen-derived routes once market design and capacity accreditation reward duration rather than nameplate megawatts. Global battery additions reached 112 GW / 307 GWh in 2025 at a 2.74-hour average duration, with China delivering roughly 54% of power and the United States about 16%, and BNEF's May 2026 outlook projects 158 GW added in 2026 rising to 308 GW in 2036 — a ~9% CAGR through the back half of the decade. BNEF NEO 2026 frames the structural trajectory as 0.223 TW of cumulative station-scale storage in 2025 rising to 3.8 TW by 2035, a greater-than-17× build in ten years, and the IEA Net Zero Emissions anchor of roughly 1,500 GW of total grid-scale storage by 2030 (about 1,200 GW batteries, supported by US$800 billion of investment) is a scenario requirement, not a forecast, and includes behind-the-meter capacity in jurisdictions unlikely to achieve it. Three findings carry the analysis. First, LFP's moat is no longer at the cell: it sits in system integration, financing, and revenue capture, and the headwind for the technology is not new chemistries but a ~71% one-year collapse in average battery revenue in ERCOT from US$192/kW-yr in 2023 to about US$55/kW-yr in 2024, with PJM's 2026/27 Base Residual Auction clearing only 5,510 MW of four-hour-class resources at a net CONE-anchored price near US$329/MW-day as the precedent for capacity-market reform. Second, non-lithium routes have cleared pilot lines but not commercial reference projects: the Sumitomo Electric / Hokkaido Electric 60 MW vanadium-redox system at Hokkaido remains the only decade-class flow reference; CATL's 40 GWh Fujian sodium-ion line is the single largest disclosed sodium expansion; and announced US sodium-ion capacity (Peak Energy, ESS Inc., Unigrid, Alsym Energy collectively ~9 GWh framework, 4 GWh 2026 plant target) is conditional offtake, not commissioned megawatt-hours. Third, duration mix is the binding constraint: China's share of new-type storage projects rated at or above four hours reached 27.6% of cumulative power at end-2025, up roughly 12 percentage points year-on-year, and 1,195 hours of equivalent utilisation across the two grids signals that dispatch discipline is replacing the prior build-and-forget mandate; outside China, the weighted-average duration remains below three hours, and that gap is where 2026–2030 capital will either migrate or stagnate. The headline forecast splits the 2030–2035 window into four mutually exclusive scenarios summing to 100%: Scenario A (Lithium Dominance, 30% probability), in which annual grid-scale additions land near or above 300 GW by 2040, LFP stays above 80% of chemistry share, and non-lithium long-duration storage remains below 20% of annual gigawatt-hours; the technology-diversification counterweight scenarios in which vanadium flow, sodium-ion, compressed-air, and thermal each carve defensible four-to-twelve-hour and multi-day niches and push non-lithium LDES share into the 30–45% range; and a regional fragmentation scenario in which interconnection-queue discipline, capacity-market reform, and policy uncertainty keep the global stock below the NEO 3.8 TW 2035 line. The basis for the scenario weights is the convergence of three independently calibrated anchors: the BNEF 2025 deployment baseline and 2026 forward outlook (S2), the IEA Net Zero requirement trajectory (S10/S11), and the LBNL observation that only about 14% of queued US capacity is ever built at a median five-year application-to-operation lead time, which acts as a global stage-discount on announced megawatt pipelines (S2/S10). The dossier's resolution indicators are explicit: at least two non-lithium chemistries must post ≥5 GWh of commissioned, non-demonstration commercial capacity in two consecutive calendar years before 2030 for the diversification scenarios to gain weight; otherwise LFP's 2025 share is the structural floor through the decade. Regional differentiation is sharp: China's 2025–2027 NDRC/NEA action plan locks a ≥180 GW new-type storage target by 2027 and reframes storage as a dispatchable system resource under Document 1360, mandating telemetry and grid-forming capability; the US faces FERC Order 2023 cluster reform and an OBBBA-reshaped Section 48 ITC against a backdrop where US 4-hour turnkey installed cost rises to roughly US$266/kWh under a 54% Section 301 tariff scenario; India carries a roughly 102 GWh tendered vs 0.5 GWh commissioned gap and a 60–83 GW 2030 requirement against a 6 GW operational base; Australia has shelved or cancelled flagship pumped-hydro projects (Mt Rawdon, Pioneer-Burdekin) at CSIRO GenCost 2025–26 benchmarks of AU$5,687/kW and AU$237/kWh for 24-hour PHES that are systematically beaten by geological, transmission, and consent overruns; and Europe, the Middle East, Africa, and Latin America each sit at distinct positions on the cost-curve-to-policy-fit map that the regional sections quantify. The four mutually exclusive scenarios, ten to twelve binary forecasts, sourced deployment and cost visualizations, and technology-share and regional policy milestone graphics that close the dossier provide the decision-grade output: a 2030 cross-over identification framework keyed to duration, technology, and jurisdiction, and a falsifier set against which each scenario can be audited as 2026–2028 data arrives.

## Executive Synthesis & Forecast Anchors (2026–2040)

### Baseline Anchors and Forecast Range, 2025 → 2040

The dossier's binding 2025 baseline is **112 GW / 307 GWh of global battery storage additions (+48% YoY), with an average duration of 2.74 h**, of which China delivered roughly 54% of power and the United States about 16% [S2]. BNEF's May 2026 forward projection is **158 GW added in 2026** rising to **308 GW in 2036**, implying a ~9% CAGR through the back half of the decade [S2]. BNEF NEO 2026 frames the structural trajectory: **0.223 TW of cumulative station-scale storage in 2025 → 3.8 TW by 2035**, a >17× build in ten years [S2]. The IEA's Net Zero Emissions scenario is the binding demand anchor: **~1,500 GW of total grid-scale storage by 2030, of which ~1,200 GW is batteries**, supported by an estimated **US$800 billion** of battery investment [S10][S11]. The NEO 3.8 TW 2035 line is broadly consistent with interpolation to IEA NZE's 2030 stock, but the IEA figure is a scenario requirement, not a forecast; it includes behind-the-meter, and a portion of NZE sits in jurisdictions unlikely to reach it (Africa, parts of Latin America).

### China: 136 GW vs. 144.7 GW — A Real Contradiction

The end-2025 Chinese cumulative stock carries an 8.7 GW gap between two official channels. The **National Energy Administration (NEA)** reports **136 GW / 351 GWh** of "new-type" storage at average 2.58 h, with lithium-ion at **96.1%** of power and compressed-air, flow, and flywheel combined at 3.9% [S68]. A separate CNESA release — itself reposted via an NEA channel — cites **144.7 GW cumulative, equal to 51.9% of the world**, implying a global stock of ~279 GW [S4]. The most plausible sources of divergence are cutoff date, "commissioned" vs "grid-connected" definition, and inclusion of demonstration projects; in any case the dossier must not silently average them. The Policy Action Plan (Document 1360) and the 2025–2027 Scale-Up Action Scheme target **≥180 GW by 2027** and RMB 250 billion of direct investment, while explicitly continuing to push compressed-air, flow, and sodium-ion toward commercialization, with solid-state, gravity, thermal, and hydrogen kept at demonstration scale [S28][S34]. End-2025 utilization averaged **1,195 hours** (State Grid 1,175 h; Southern Grid 1,294 h) — a 300-hour jump that signals improving dispatch, but utilization alone does not prove project-level IRR [S68].

### United States, Europe, and the Rest

United States utility-scale additions were **10.4 GW in 2024** with **>26 GW cumulative** [S62], rising to **~50 GW utility-scale cumulative and 65 GW including front-of-meter by end of 2025** and an EIA-surveyed 19.6 GW planned for 2025 [S62][S42]. SEIA/Benchmark put the US on a path to **≥600 GWh cumulative by 2030**, anchored by 28 GW / 57 GWh added in 2025 [S2]. LBNL's queue data, however, is the single most important caveat: **1,030 GW of storage sits in interconnection queues** alongside 1,570 GW of generation, with average cycles from application to commercial operation now **~5 years** (up from <2 in 2008), a ~14% capacity-completion rate historically, and roughly 11,600 active projects [S2]. In Europe, EASE's Q2 2026 *European Market Monitor on Energy Storage* crossed a symbolic threshold when cumulative storage capacity overtook nuclear capacity, after **100 GW cum by November 2025 and a projected +115% growth by 2030** [S2]. A leaked EU Electrification Action Plan pointed to a **~200 GW European storage requirement by 2030**, against only ~55 GW in operation or scheduled by end-2026 — leaving substantial pipeline but no formal mandate [S2].

### LCOS Anchor: Lazard v19 (July 2026)

Lazard LCOE+ v19 is the dossier's binding 4-hour LCOS anchor: **US$210–292/MWh unsubsidized**, narrowing to **US$148–209/MWh** with the Investment Tax Credit — a nominal jump from v18 driven by tariff scenarios rather than fundamental cost increases [S35][S36][S77]. These numbers must be read with discipline: they are not directly comparable to CSIRO GenCost 2025–26 (AU$385/kWh for utility 4-hr BESS in Australia) or to BNEF's 2025 global 4-hr LCOE of US$78/MWh, because the Lazard figure embeds US project development, tariff baselines, and explicit financing terms, whereas BNEF's number is closer to a survey median without a uniform discount rate [S2]. Any LCOS table in the dossier that compares regions or technologies must hold discharge duration, equivalent full cycles, charge cost, round-trip efficiency, discount rate, augmentation, and capacity-credit revenue constant [S35][S36].

### Analyst Interpolation — Explicit

The dossier's working grid-scale deployment table, anchored at 112 GW added in 2025 (observed), 158 GW added in 2026 (BNEF), and 308 GW added in 2036 (BNEF), interpolates to **~184 GW in 2030** and **~236 GW in 2035** at the all-battery level, and applies a working assumption of **~80% of power and ~85% of energy** being grid-scale or large front-of-meter, with average duration rising from 2.74 h (2025) to **3.4 h (2030), 4.0 h (2035), and 5.2 h (2040)** — a fragile assumption, not observed data. Under those working assumptions the grid-scale cumulative stock tracks **~0.22 TW (2025) → ~1.0 TW (2030) → ~2.1 TW (2035) → ~3.4 TW (2040)** in power terms. If the grid-scale share is 70% instead of 80%, the 2040 power figure falls to ~3.0 TW; if it is 90%, it rises to ~3.8 TW — i.e., it converges on the BNEF NEO 2035 line shifted five years out [S2].

### Where the Dossier Stands to Be Wrong

Three contradictions must be carried into the rest of the dossier rather than averaged away. (1) **NEA 136 GW vs. CNESA 144.7 GW at end-2025** — same regulator, different channels; do not collapse. (2) **IEA NZE 1,500 GW by 2030 vs. BNEF NEO 3.8 TW by 2035** — the IEA number is a scenario requirement (mostly batteries), while BNEF tracks expected build under stated policy; the dossier's base case sits between them. (3) **Lazard v19 $210–292/MWh vs. BNEF global 4-hr LCOE ~US$78/MWh** — definitional, not contradictory; Lazard is US turnkey with explicit ITC and tariff assumptions, BNEF is a survey median for grid-scale projects.

The base-case deployment trajectory assumes the **LFP/4-hr regime remains the bulk market through 2030**, with sodium-ion gaining 4-hr cost parity by 2028–2031 but rarely displacing LFP in stationary use, while non-lithium long-duration begins to surface at scale post-2030 only if contracts resolvable in dollar terms appear (see §13). The remaining sections — technologies (LFP, sodium-ion, flow, thermal/CAES, hydro, hydrogen), supply chain and recycling, interconnection, market design, safety, regional deep dives, project economics, scenarios, and binary forecasts — all sit on these anchors without re-deriving them.

## Lithium Iron Phosphate: Cost Curve, Manufacturing Scale & Defensible Moat

### Cost curve: from $1,400/kWh to the $140 floor and what 2026–2040 really buys

Lithium iron phosphate's defining commercial fact is a roughly 90% real price collapse in thirteen years: lithium-ion battery packs fell from about US$1,400/kWh in 2010 to below US$140/kWh in 2023, with LFP cell chemistry accounting for roughly 80% of new battery storage additions globally by 2023 [S10][S11]. By 2025 LFP's share of Chinese new-type storage installations had climbed to 96.1% [S68], and BNEF's 2025 global 4-hour battery LCOE was reported near US$78/MWh, down 27% year-on-year [S2]. The cost story is therefore no longer "can cells get cheaper?" but "can installed system cost, financing, and revenue catch up to the cell?" — a question that increasingly sits outside the LFP supply chain.

### Manufacturing scale, integration dominance, and the shipment-to-installed gap

Chinese cell makers already hold roughly 85% of global cell-manufacturing capacity [S10][S11]. Wood Mackenzie puts Chinese system integrators at about 76% of the global BESS market in 2025, with eight of the top ten integrators Chinese [S33]. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence's 2025 shipment data — circulated via industry press — show about 600 GWh of BESS-grade cells shipped against roughly 460 GWh of systems and around 320 GWh installed [S33]. The 2026 nameplate cell capacity is reported at about 1.7 TWh [S33]. That gap is the section's most important non-price fact: announced capacity, cell shipments, system shipments, and operating capacity are four different numbers, and conflating them is the most common error in storage forecasts.

### Defensible moat: why LFP holds the 4-hour slot through 2040

LFP's moat in 4-hour applications is self-reinforcing. The same supply chain that produced the 90% cost decline also produces the warranty history, financing templates, EPC reference set, and integration know-how that bankers discount. BNEF's 54% tariff scenario still leaves US 4-hour turnkey near US$266/kWh — a 30% premium to a no-tariff base — but does not displace LFP, it reprices it [S2]. Australian 4-hour turnkey sits around AU$385/kWh and 24-hour battery at AU$266/kWh on CSIRO's GenCost 2025–26 deck, with pumped hydro at AU$237/kWh but AU$5,687/kW of power capex [S72][S36]. Lazard's June 2025 LCOE+ reaffirmed declining US storage costs, even after pandemic-era upticks [S36][S77]. The defensible moat, in short, is the integrated cost of building, financing, insuring, and dispatching a 4-hour asset — not the cell alone.

### Where the moat narrows: duration extension, safety, and concentration risk

LFP's defensibility weakens as duration extends. Eight-hour LFP stacks the same per-cycle power electronics and balance-of-plant against a longer energy block, and the marginal kWh is fully exposed to graphite, lithium processing, and LFP-material cost — supply nodes where Chinese share remains dominant. Thermal, compressed-air, and flow alternatives attack this energy-cost slope directly. DOE Liftoff frames the trade-off: intra-day LDES targets of US$650/kW and 75% round-trip efficiency versus current bests near US$1,100–1,400/kW and 69% RTE; multi-day LDES at US$1,100/kW and 55–60% RTE versus US$1,900–2,500/kW and 45% RTE today [S88]. These are targets, not delivered costs. Safety is the second moat-narrower: the Moss Landing fire one year on continues to shape NFPA 855's 2026 edition and California siting rules [S39][S74]. LFP's lower thermal-runaway risk is real, but it is also becoming a regulatory floor, not a differentiator.

### Capacity credit and the 8-hour question

The LFP-versus-LDES contest is increasingly decided by capacity-market design, not hardware. China's official push toward independent storage earning energy, ancillary, and capacity compensation revenue [S34] is the live test of whether 8-hour LFP can stack enough revenue to justify its energy capex. ERCOT battery revenue collapsed from about US$192/kW-yr in 2023 to about US$55/kW-yr in 2024 — a 71% drop — illustrating that LFP's 4-hour arbitrage moat can be eaten by its own prior entrants [S22]. NREL's 2024 ATB treats duration extension as a cost-and-revenue lever, not a free option [S30][S64]. The defensible LFP position through 2040 is therefore: dominant in 4-hour, contested at 8-hour, marginal at 12-hour and beyond — and that is the axis the scenarios below use.

### Section-level takeaway

LFP's cost curve is no longer the binding constraint on storage deployment — interconnection queues, revenue cannibalization, and duration-mix policy are. The defensible moat through 2040 is the 4-hour system, not the cell. Scenario A (48%) is the working prior; Scenario D (6%) is the tail risk that requires both a capacity-market redesign and a deployment stall simultaneously.

## Sodium-Ion: Commercialization Reality Check vs Announced Capacity

Sodium-ion's defensible value proposition is not yet a cost-per-kWh race with LFP — it is a *physical-fit* and *policy-fit* story. Sodium cells can be shipped at zero-volt state, transported and stored without thermal-runaway risk under mechanical abuse, and operated across a wider temperature window than LFP, which is why US and European promoters lead with safety, cold-climate performance, and supply-chain diversification rather than $/kWh [S52]. CATL's 40 GWh Fujian sodium-ion line, announced at roughly US$735 million, is the single largest disclosed sodium expansion to date and signals that Chinese cell leadership is hedging between LFP and sodium rather than abandoning either [S40]. HiNa Battery's transition to multi-GWh commercial shipments confirms that the chemistry has cleared pilot lines [S44].

**Announced capacity vs shipped product.** The gap is large and is the single most important quantitative check on the narrative. Peak Energy, ESS Inc., Unigrid, and Alsym Energy have collectively disclosed roughly a 9 GWh framework and a 4 GWh 2026 plant target in the United States [S52]. These are conditional offtake and capacity announcements, not delivered, commissioned, and dispatched megawatt-hours. To treat any of these as firm utility-scale deployment is to repeat the 2022–2023 mistake made on early LFP gigafactory announcements, where nameplate capacity and operating capacity diverged by a factor of three or more during ramp. A defensible 2026 figure for global sodium-ion BESS shipments into grid-scale service is best characterized as a low-to-mid single-digit GWh, against a roughly 600 GWh global BESS cell-shipment base that LFP dominates [S33].

**Where sodium is most likely to displace LFP first.** Three use cases align with the chemistry's actual advantages and sidestep the energy-density penalty:

1. *Cold-climate and high-latitude installations* — sodium cells lose less capacity at sub-zero temperatures, eliminating the HVAC load and parasitic draw that LFP systems require in northern US, Canadian, and Nordic sites; here the relevant benchmark is $/kWh *delivered in winter*, not $/kWh nameplate.
2. *Behind-the-meter and commercial/industrial* — zero-volt transport removes hazmat classification for shipping, simplifying module replacement cycles and reducing insurance and permitting friction.
3. *Light-duty freight, ports, and microgrids* — where duty cycles are shallow and weight is not the binding constraint, sodium's lower nominal energy density is a smaller handicap.

For 4-hour utility-scale service in temperate climates, sodium must clear three commercial bars before it is treated as a credible LFP substitute: (a) at least 100 MWh of contracted, commissioned projects with third-party efficiency and availability data, not vendor bulletins; (b) system installed cost within roughly 15% of equivalent LFP turnkey in the same ISO market; and (c) bankable warranty terms comparable to LFP's emerging 15-year performance guarantees. None of these has been demonstrated at utility scale as of mid-2026.

**The 2028–2031 window for partial cost-parity testing.** This is the realistic inflection window. Three observable signals will determine whether sodium crosses into utility-scale credibility. First, whether the 2026 Peak/Alsym/Unigrid 4 GWh plant line and follow-on capacity actually ship customer-accepted cells in 2027 rather than slipping into engineering samples. Second, whether at least two independent system integrators outside China release sodium-based 20-foot container products priced within striking distance of the current BNEF-reported 4-hour LCOE reference near US$78/MWh [S61] — note that this LCOE is not a like-for-like LCOS and the comparison must adjust for cycle life, charging cost, and discount rate. Third, whether any US interconnection queue project after 2028 lists sodium chemistry in its IRA-qualifying component disclosure; FEOC and domestic-content rules, combined with the 2025 Lazard observation that US storage cost reductions in 2025 offset prior pandemic-era increases [S77], give sodium a localized pricing wedge in FEOC-restricted procurements even if global cell costs remain above LFP.

**Decision-relevant contradiction.** The bullish case treats sodium as a structural LFP substitute by 2030; the contrarian case, supported by the announcement-vs-shipment gap and the absence of any 100 MWh-class sodium reference project with third-party operating data, treats it as a niche chemistry that captures cold-climate, microgrid, and behind-the-meter segments while remaining a rounding error in utility-scale GWh terms through 2030. The base case in this dossier is closer to the contrarian reading: sodium-ion is more likely to be a 1–3% share of global grid-scale BESS shipments in 2028, rising toward 5–8% only if the cost-parity tests above are met. The 2040 share is governed by the global scenarios section; this section does not re-derive it.

**What would update this view.** A sodium-cell LCOS within roughly US$15/MWh of LFP at identical duration and cycle count, demonstrated in a publicly filed utility procurement; a 100 MWh-plus commissioned project in a cold-climate ISO with operating data; or a Chinese provincial policy mandating sodium content in new BESS projects, similar to how domestic-content rules have shaped US LFP.

## Flow Batteries: Vanadium, Iron, Zinc — Commercial Reference Projects & Financing

### Why flow batteries still don't have a "Sumitomo class" reference

The Sumitomo Electric / Hokkaido Electric 60 MW / 252 MWh Yusaku vanadium-redox battery — built in 2015 and expanded in 2022 to 60 MW / 510 MWh — remains the only large-scale flow battery that has operated continuously for close to a decade and demonstrated decoupled power/energy architecture at utility scale [S49]. The reference class matters: 4 years from demo to commercial, with vendor-warranted electrolyte replenishment and a stack life that the user does not have to amortize. No iron-flow, zinc-bromine, or zinc-iron project yet matches that operating record, and the LCOS evidence gap remains the dominant constraint on bankability.

### LCOS evidence gap is the binding problem

Lazard's June 2024 LCOE+ release and NREL's 2024b ATB both publish LCOS curves for utility-scale battery storage, but both are chemistry-blended, dominated by lithium-ion cost curves, and do not isolate flow batteries at the 100 MWh-plus scale [S35][S30]. NREL's 2025 update narrows the data but continues to flag flow batteries as a low-confidence row [S37]. The IEA's *Batteries and Secure Energy Transitions* and executive summary similarly treat non-lithium long-duration storage as a separate policy segment rather than a cost-competing technology in 2026–2028 [S10][S11]. Industry coverage reinforces the asymmetry: even the bullish Liftoff report acknowledges that long-duration storage "is increasingly competitive but unlikely to match Li-ion's cost reductions" on a near-term basis [S72][S88]. The implication is that any 2026–2030 flow LCOS claim sourced from a vendor is closer to a target than to an observed figure.

### Vanadium pricing risk remains the chemistry-specific exposure

Vanadium pentoxide spot pricing has been volatile enough historically that several banks will not underwrite vanadium-redox projects without electrolyte floor-price hedges or vendor buyback agreements. That risk is not theoretical: if V2O5 prices spike in a tight steel-market year, the electrolyte replacement cost over a 20-year project life can exceed the entire power-side capex. Iron-flow chemistries eliminate that commodity exposure but substitute a different uncertainty — the round-trip efficiency ceiling of roughly 70–75% versus 75–80% for vanadium, and a still-thin operating dataset. Zinc-based flow chemistries (zinc-bromine, zinc-iron) offer higher energy density and lower-cost stack materials, but dendrite formation and cycle-life degradation in field conditions remain vendor-controlled disclosures rather than third-party verified.

### China demonstration programs set a 2026–2028 floor

CNESA's H1 2025 tracking shows China added 23.03 GW / 56.12 GWh of new-type storage in the first half alone, taking cumulative installed capacity past 100 GW [S31], and year-end coverage confirms a 2025 total near 130 GW of new capacity with lithium-ion at 96.1% of the mix [S9][S32]. The NDRC's September 2025 *New-Type Storage Scaling Action Plan* explicitly calls for compressed-air, flow, and sodium-ion to move toward commercialization in 2025–2027, while still anchoring the 180 GW-plus deployment target on lithium-ion [S34][S68]. The Plan's language treats flow batteries as a *demonstration-to-commercial* segment, not as a 2026–2028 capacity contributor at scale. That regulatory framing — capacity scale on lithium, commercial readiness on flow — is consistent with how the Sumitomo reference class actually emerged in Japan.

### What a credible 2028–2032 reference project looks like

For a flow project to set the kind of reference class that unlocks project finance without concessional debt, it would need at minimum: 100 MW nameplate with at least 4 hours (400 MWh) and decoupled power/energy sizing; third-party verified round-trip efficiency measured over 12+ months; vendor-warranted availability above 90% and capacity retention above 95% at the warranted cycle count; a published augmentation cost per kWh for a 20-year horizon; and either an electrolyte floor-price hedge or a vendor electrolyte buyback. The first US Department of Energy long-duration storage funding selections in 2023–2024 financed several flow projects with exactly that disclosure regime in their award terms [S49], but those projects had not entered commercial operation at the time of available reporting [S72]. Form Energy's iron-air technology — distinct from flow but often grouped with it in policy frameworks — is targeting a 100-hour duration and has begun limited commercial deployment [S93][S98].

### Architecture advantage vs. operating-data deficit

The structural argument for flow batteries at 8–12 hours is real: power and energy scale independently, so marginal duration costs only the electrolyte plus tankage, not additional power conversion. Lithium-ion at 8 hours stacks a second full power block against the same energy, doubling power-side capex per kWh. That asymmetry should, in theory, let flow win below a cycle-cost crossover. But theory has not yet produced a vendor with a published, bank-grade LCOS for a 100 MWh-plus operating project in 2026. Until that disclosure exists, the most defensible position is that flow batteries are the best-architecture long-duration candidate without a Sumitomo-class commercial reference — and the probability that one emerges before 2030 depends primarily on whether the DOE Liftoff demonstration cohort delivers operating data on schedule [S88].

## Thermal, Compressed-Air & Liquid-Air Storage: Technology-by-Technology Contrarian Audit

### Thermal storage: heat hosts, not yet grid resources

Thermal storage for grid applications splits cleanly into two commercial logics: thermal-to-thermal for industrial heat hosts, and thermal-to-power for the much harder task of re-electrifying stored heat. The 2025–2027 China NDRC/NEA action plan explicitly classifies compressed air, flow, and sodium-ion as routes to be commercialized, while solid-state, gravity, thermal, and hydrogen storage are placed in the demonstration track [S34]. That taxonomy alone signals where Chinese regulators see commercial readiness as of September 2025.

Antora Energy (US thermophotovoltaic brick), Malta (pumped heat), Energy Dome (CO2 thermobaric), and Brenmiller (crusite) all sit at TRL 7–9 for thermal-to-thermal, with pilots sized tens of MWh [S14]. The headwind is that grid operators are not paid for industrial heat; they are paid for energy and ancillary services. Projects that anchor on a cement, steel, aluminum, or process-steam off-taker can monetize without round-trip efficiency (RTE) penalties, which is why Brenmiller and Energy Vault's hybrid thermal-plus-battery pitch targets behind-the-meter host sites first. The contrarian read: thermal storage will scale where it competes with thermal gas boilers, not where it competes with lithium. The DOE Liftoff framing of long-duration storage (LDES) implicitly assumes the same — capacity revenue is the enabler, not thermal arbitrage on its own [S14].

For thermal-to-power, round-trip efficiency depends on a topping or bottoming cycle, and the working efficiency band is roughly 40–55% for the credible designs [S14]. DOE's 2030 multi-day target requires US$1,100/kW at 55–60% RTE; current best multi-day thermal-to-power sits well above US$1,900/kW at roughly 45% RTE [S14]. Reaching the target requires capacity-market monetization plus at least one ≥100 MW commercial reference project per vendor with a third-party operating record. Until that exists, thermal-to-power remains a demonstration-class bet.

### Compressed-air storage: salt caverns are the moat, not the cycle

Diabatic CAES (the Huntorf 1978 and McIntosh 1991 reference units) delivers roughly 42–55% RTE by burning natural gas during expansion; the modern Hydrostor Apex (Anglo American, 300 MW / 2,100 MWh, approved 2024) replaces that with compressed air plus thermal management and targets ~60–65% RTE. Hydrostor's commercial model rests entirely on salt-cavern siting: cheap air storage, low leakage, and a regulatory class already familiar to oil-and-gas permitting. The Huntorf→McIntosh 13-year gap between operating commercial CAES units is itself the reference class — there were no grid-scale CAES builds in North America between 1991 and the early 2020s [S14].

Adiabatic CAES (A-CAES) adds a thermal store so no fuel is burned during discharge. The unproven piece is the diurnal/seasonal thermal store at scale; vendors including Hydrostor and LightSail claim component validation, but no unit has run ≥1,000 equivalent full cycles under independent measurement. Siting flexibility — A-CAES does not strictly require a salt cavern if above-ground pressure vessels are used — is often cited as a moat, but the cost premium versus cavern storage is the binding constraint.

For a commercial reference project ≤US$1,100/kW by 2030 (DOE multi-day target), the credible scenarios are (i) Hydrostor or a Tier-1 EPC delivers an A-CAES unit at known salt-cavern sites (e.g., Saskatchewan, Texas Permian, North Sea) with a 15-year tolling or capacity contract, or (ii) a Chinese developer — which has been told by NDRC/NEA to commercialize CAES as part of the 180 GW by-2027 plan [S34] — produces a 100 MW+ unit at a defined capex. Until either happens, CAES sits below thermal-to-thermal on the commercial-readiness ladder.

### Liquid-air and CO2 cycles: medium-density, medium-evidence

Liquid-air energy storage (LAES) — Highview Power's design, with commercial pilots at Carrington (UK, 50 MW demonstrator) and a 300 MW project under development in the UK with the government's cap-and-floor mechanism — uses off-the-shelf turbo-machinery, so the vendor pitch is supply-chain resilience and modular siting. Round-trip efficiency sits in the 50–60% band, with the gap to adiabatic CAES being the liquefaction penalty. CO2-cycle variants (Energy Dome's CO2 battery) reduce siting constraints by using standard pressure vessels rather than cryogenic tanks, at the cost of a longer pathway to third-party validated cycle counts.

The decisive bottleneck for LAES and CO2 cycles is not the thermodynamics but the warranty record. No commercial unit has accumulated the 1,000–2,000 annual cycles over 10+ years that debt financiers demand for non-recourse project finance at investment-grade spreads. The Highview UK cap-and-floor contract effectively substitutes regulator-backed revenue for missing operating history [S14]; without analogous structures in the US or other markets, LAES/CO2 deployment beyond a handful of reference units is unlikely before the early 2030s.

### Where thermal, CAES and LAES actually fit by 2030

| Technology | Best-fit duration | Siting constraint | TRL | Binding constraint to ≤US$1,100/kW by 2030 |

The maximum credible non-lithium share of global grid-scale annual new GWh by 2030 remains in the low single digits, as flagged in the technology-competition section. Thermal, CAES, and LAES win by occupying siting and duration niches that lithium cannot, not by competing head-on.

## Pumped Hydro & Hydrogen-Derived Storage: Mature Techs, New Headwinds

### Pumped hydro: a mature technology with new headwinds

Global pumped hydro storage (PHES) added only **8.4 GW in 2024**, of which **92% was in China** [S2]. Yet the International Hydropower Association tracks roughly **600 GW of pipeline PHES**, against a realised build rate of about **8 GW per year** [S2]. That gap — a 75× ratio between announced and built — is the central headwind. Three Australian cases make the pattern concrete. The Mt Rawdon **2 GW/20 GWh** scheme was shelved by the Queensland government [S2]; Borumba has inflated to **AU$18.4 bn** for **2 GW/48 GWh**; and Pioneer-Burdekin, the largest announced PHES in the Southern Hemisphere, was cancelled outright. Each failure was preceded by independent engineering reviews showing that benchmark installed costs (CSIRO GenCost 2025–26 lists 24-h PHES at roughly **AU$5,687/kW** and **AU$237/kWh**) are systematically beaten by geological surprise, transmission route cost, indigenous consent timelines, and political exposure to single-project overruns.

The Australian pattern mirrors global experience. LBNL finds US interconnection queues now exceed **1,030 GW of storage**, but only about **14%** of queued capacity is ever built and the average project takes **~5 years** from application to operation [S10]. PHES sits at the slow end of that distribution because its civil works are not modular. The implication for the 2026–2040 forecast horizon is that PHES growth will continue to come predominantly from China — where the State Grid and Southern Power Grid can compress siting, civil works, and grid connection inside provincial planning cycles — and that the IHA's ~600 GW pipeline should be treated as an upper bound that will be progressively cancelled rather than built. For the 8–36 h duration tier in particular, PHES will increasingly compete with 8-h LFP projects (Chinese policy already requires 4-h-or-longer projects to reach **27.6%** of new-build power in 2025, up roughly 12 percentage points in a year [S68]) rather than with shorter batteries.

### Hydrogen-derived storage: structural RTE penalty and the asset-sharing workaround

Pure power-to-hydrogen-to-power carries a structural **30–40% round-trip efficiency** penalty because electrolysis (65–75%), compression or liquefaction, storage losses, and either a fuel cell (50–60%) or a hydrogen turbine (35–45%) compound multiplicatively [S96]. That compares with **75%+** for LFP at 4 h, and the DOE's intra-day LDES target of **75% RTE** at **US$650/kW** by 2030 [S88]. Even at DOE's multi-day LDES target of **55–60% RTE** and **US$1,100/kW** [S88], hydrogen must beat battery capex on an energy-only basis by a factor that historical cost curves do not support.

The economically defensible route is therefore **asset-sharing with industrial hydrogen offtake**. Under this design, the electrolyser runs primarily against chemical, steel, refining, or ammonia offtakers; the storage tank and reconversion unit (fuel cell or turbine) are sized only for grid-cycling residuals, and the project's IRR is anchored to long-term hydrogen offtake contracts rather than to power arbitrage. The IEA's Global Hydrogen Review 2024 frames hydrogen storage as a flexibility asset for power systems, but explicitly couples it to industrial demand build-out [S96]. Where that industrial demand exists — North West Europe, the US Gulf Coast, parts of the Gulf, Western Australia — power-to-X-with-storage becomes plausible. Where it does not — most of Latin America, Africa outside South Africa, Southeast Asia — pure power-to-hydrogen-to-power remains a niche with high deliverable LCOS.

### Cross-cutting implications

The two technologies share an identical demand-side gating factor: **the willingness of system planners and regulators to contract for duration at all**. PHES still wins on round-trip efficiency and asset life (50–80 years), but its unit cost is exposed to single-project shocks in a way that modular chemistries are not. Hydrogen-derived storage is symmetric: cheap at energy scale, but its round-trip efficiency and capacity factor penalty are structural rather than transitional. Both will grow between 2026 and 2040 only where explicit capacity payments, mandate-style procurement, or industrial-anchor offtake structures reduce merchant exposure. Where those structures are absent — most of Africa, parts of Latin America, and merchant-only US sub-regions — both PHES and P2P2P will continue to under-deliver against announced pipelines, leaving 8–12-h battery and thermal storage as the default multi-day option (see Section 13 for the duration-competition synthesis).

## Supply Chain, Raw Materials & Recycling: Lithium, Graphite, Vanadium, Sodium

### China supply-chain concentration: scale and risk surface

China controls roughly half of global lithium and cobalt processing and approximately 85% of cell manufacturing, with European, US, and Korean producers each holding no more than about 10% share in any major node [S10]. Chinese system integrators captured roughly 76% of the global BESS market in 2025, with eight of the top ten integrators headquartered in China [S33]. Benchmark data reported by industry media place 2025 BESS cell shipments near 600 GWh and system shipments near 460 GWh against installed volumes of approximately 320 GWh; 2026 nameplate cell capacity could reach about 1.7 TWh [S33]. The widening gap between nameplate capacity, shipments, and installations is a binding signal: it implies inventory build, project slippage, and a deflationary cost floor that protects Chinese cost leadership but pressures warranty reserves and integrator margins.

LFP's chemistry shift has displaced the traditional "lithium-ion will hit cobalt/nickel bottlenecks" argument. The residual concentration risk in LFP now sits with graphite (anode), refined lithium, lithium iron phosphate cathode precursor, and power-conversion systems and integrators. 2025 China new-type storage reached 136 GW/351 GWh with average duration 2.58 hours, and lithium-ion accounted for 96.1% of cumulative power [S68]. Even if vanadium and zinc-bromide flows double their share annually from a low base, they will not materially change Chinese or global supply concentration through 2030.

### Trade-policy shocks: tariffs, FEOC, IRA, Section 301

BNEF estimates that a 54% Section 301 tariff scenario raises US 4-hour turnkey cost to about US$266/kWh, about 30% above the no-tariff baseline, and its high-tariff case trims 2025–2027 US buildout by 51%–74% [S2]. These are scenario outputs, not observed deployment declines, but they indicate that the policy lever is large enough to redirect supply chains if sustained. The 2025 OBBBA amendments tightened IRA domestic-content and Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) restrictions, reshaping eligibility for the Section 45X manufacturing credit and 48E investment credit [S25][S86]. FEOC rules effectively force US project sponsors and cell makers to decertify Chinese-controlled suppliers across anode, cathode, and pack stages, which is operationally difficult given China's upstream position.

The European response layers on carbon-footprint, recycled-content, and due-diligence obligations under the EU Battery Regulation 2023/1542, phased in from 2025, with industrial storage batteries facing stronger data and end-of-life requirements [S92]. Combined with the Net-Zero Industry Act target for 40% local manufacturing by 2030, Europe is constructing a regulatory perimeter that raises the cost of Chinese system imports even where duties are unchanged [S92]. Net effect: trade policy is fragmenting the previously global LFP cost curve into at least three regional price regimes.

### Sodium-ion: precursor feasibility vs. competitive cost

Sodium-ion's value proposition rests on lithium-free precursors (alumina-coated precursors, Prussian-blue analogues, hard carbon), zero-volt shipping, wider temperature windows, and reduced fire risk, not immediate energy-cost parity with LFP [S52]. CATL's announced 40 GWh Fujian sodium-ion expansion is a capacity plan, not a run-rate shipment; HiNa Battery's commercialisation claims remain early-cycle [S40][S44]. Western sodium-ion ventures such as Peak Energy, ESS Inc., Unigrid, and Alsym have signed memoranda and a 9 GWh framework agreement for mining applications, but these are forward orders rather than operating data [S52]. Without multi-year operational evidence at ≥100 MWh, sodium-ion's competitive claim is most defensible in safety-constrained or low-temperature niches rather than grid-scale energy markets before 2028.

### Vanadium and flow-battery precursors

Vanadium pentoxide supply is geographically concentrated: China, Russia, South Africa, and Brazil dominate mine output, with China holding the majority of electrolyte processing capacity. Vanadium redox-flow batteries (VRFBs) decouple power and energy, providing long cycle life (often 15,000–20,000 cycles), but face higher round-trip efficiency penalties, parasitic loads from pumps and stacks, and electrolyte financing that requires off-take or leasing structures to be project-financeable. Iron-flow and zinc-bromide chemistries sidestep vanadium price risk but inherit similar integration challenges. Through 2030, VRFB growth is more likely constrained by project pipeline conversion and electrolyte leasing standardisation than by mine output.

### Recycling economics: 2030 displacement question

By 2030 recycling is more likely to relieve nickel and cobalt demand pressure and modestly supplement lithium supply rather than replace new mining for grid-scale LFP [S10]. LFP's low residual metal value makes hydrometallurgical recycling uneconomic at small scale; transport, dismantling, and black-mass processing costs can exceed recovered material value without producer responsibility schemes or regulatory floors. EU Battery Regulation mandates recycled-content thresholds and collection targets that will pull recycling volumes upward, but raise system cost [S92]. The headline figure for the scenarios section must therefore be flagged: 2030 recycling offsets mining demand only partially, concentrated in NMC chemistries and regions with mandated take-back.

### Supply-chain risk transmission to deployment forecasts

Supply-chain concentration interacts with regional market design. Where trade barriers lift local prices (US), interconnection queues and revenue cannibalisation (ERCOT ancillary-services revenue fell from about US$192/kW-yr in 2023 to about US$55/kW-yr in 2024) become the binding constraint on deployment rather than equipment availability [S62]. In Europe, the bottleneck is shifting from cell supply to grid-connection queues (over €100 billion of wind and battery projects stalled across eight member states) and double network charges. In China, supply-chain scale is enabling the 2027 ≥180 GW target, but the 2025–2027 action plan signals a shift from mandated build to dispatchable, market-reimbursed operation [S34].

Resolution indicators for this section: (1) non-Chinese suppliers shipping ≥100 GWh/yr BESS without Chinese IP or material dependency by 2030; (2) at least two non-lithium 10-hour-plus technologies each reaching ≥1 GW cumulative commercial operation by 2030; (3) EU Battery Regulation recycled-content thresholds triggering demonstrable secondary-material supply for new LFP cells by 2028.

## Grid Interconnection, Curtailment & Project Lead Times

### US interconnection queue: signal-to-noise of pipeline data

LBNL's *Queued Up* 2024 edition counts roughly 1,030 GW of storage in the US interconnection queue at end-2023 against about 11,600 active generation and storage requests [S2]. Queue length is "interest" not "future deployment": the median time from interconnection application to commercial operation has risen from under two years in 2008 to roughly five years by 2023, and historically only about 20% of projects / 14% of proposed capacity reach COD [S2]. Pulling a single GW figure from the queue without a stage-discount systematically overstates build by roughly an order of magnitude.

### FERC Order 2023 and clustering

The 2023 interconnection final rule moved ISO/RTOs from a first-come, first-served serial study to cluster studies, with tighter penalties for withdrawals and clearer cluster-closure milestones [S84]. In MISO, PJM, and CAISO this is already shortening median time-to-IS for projects that survive cluster entry, but the order does not solve withdrawal gaming: developers now over-submit clusters, then drop low-marginal-cost sites once network-upgrade cost-sharing is allocated. Net effect is fewer but better-screened projects, with material delays for any applicant not pre-positioned with site control, network modeling, and capital. Storage is disproportionately affected because its interconnection cost depends on nearby renewables whose deliverability often drives the upgrade bill.

### ISO/RTO behaviour

ERCOT sits outside FERC Order 2023 (it is not an RTO under FERC jurisdiction), so its fast cycle and 71% battery-revenue compression between 2023 (≈US$192/kW-yr) and 2024 (≈US$55/kW-yr) is driven by supply rather than rule design [S2]. CAISO is the test bed for the cluster regime and the Western RA/ELCC framework [S83]. PJM's 2024–2025 capacity accreditation reform is the most direct lever on storage duration economics because lower-capacity-credit hours re-rank 4h against 10–12h assets. MISO's seasonal auction (春秋三季) and queue reform together set the upper bound on what an LFP cluster can earn during summer peaks.

### China: dispatch-telemetry gating

LFP hardware in China can be installed in months, but a project only becomes a system resource once dispatch telemetry passes grid-code conformance and market-registration tests. The 2025–2027 Action Plan requires storage stations to be "observable, measurable, controllable" and to follow dispatch commands, with non-compliant assets removed from market registration [S34]. Implementation means a project's true lead time is set by telemetry, market access, and provincial acceptance rather than by supply. Regional curtailment data remain discontinuous: NEA reports annual national equivalent utilization hours (about 1,195 h in 2025) but does not publish province-level charge/discharge price separation, so developers cannot verify revenue mechanics from public data alone [S34].

### EU distribution-queue bottleneck

The EU's binding constraint has migrated from cell supply to distribution-grid access. Industry reporting cites roughly €100 bn of wind and battery projects stalled across eight member-state distribution queues [S2]. The Net-Zero Industry Act's 12–18 month permit target applies to manufacturing and selected storage authorizations, not to network-connection offers. Until DSO-level connection charges, capacity allocation, and dual-use rights (storage + flexibility + grid service) are standardized across TSOs/DSOs, lead times in Spain, Germany, Italy, and Ireland will stay in the 4–7 year range even when batteries ship in 6 months [S11].

### India: DISCOM-level interconnection

India's 2025 tenders cleared about 102 GWh yet commissioned under 0.5 GWh [S2]. The wedge between tender and COD sits at the state DISCOM interface: charge-point of-connection studies, deviation-settlement-account exposure, and 15-year offtaker credit risk all feed back into bid pricing. Until SECI and the states harmonize connection-charge methodology and waiver retroactive open-access charges for storage charging, tender volumes will continue to overstate deployment by roughly two orders of magnitude.

### Lead times by technology

For grid-forming LFP the hardware-installation box is roughly 6–18 months; utility interconnect and market registration extend total project lead time to 3–6 years in the US and 4–7 years in parts of the EU. Long-duration and pumped hydro push 7–12 years, dominated by licensing and construction, not supply. Sodium-ion and flow are roughly LFP on hardware but have no permitting track record.

### Curtailment data: decision-useful vs current disclosure

For curtailment metrics to be decision-useful they must report (a) annual MWh curtailed and % of potential generation by ISO/control area; (b) hourly price separation between high-renewables hours and evening peak; (c) state-of-charge and dispatch behavior of co-located storage; (d) counterfactual without storage. CAISO, ERCOT, MISO, EirGrid, and NESO currently publish partial elements on different cadences; without consistent reporting, "curtailment falling" cannot be distinguished from "battery soak" absorbing curtailment invisible to public data [S10].

## Market Design: Capacity Markets, Ancillary Services & Revenue Cannibalization

### Capacity markets, ancillary services, and revenue cannibalization as the binding constraint on the 2026–2040 build-out

Market design now does more than revenue stacking; it has become the primary gate on whether storage, particularly at 8h, 12h, and multi-day durations, can pay back its capital. The ERCOT trajectory is the canonical evidence: average battery revenue collapsed from roughly US$192/kW-yr in 2023 to about US$55/kW-yr in 2024, a ~71% one-year decline, as fleet-scale additions simultaneously pushed ancillary services and arbitrage margins toward their marginal-fuel-cost floor (Modo Energy, 2025) [S75]. Capacity-market reform is the device through which system operators attempt to monetize duration that energy-only and ancillary markets structurally underpay. PJM's 2026/27 Base Residual Auction cleared 5,510 MW of 4-hr-class ELRP resources at a net CONE-anchored price near US$329/MW-day (PJM, 2026) [S82]; ISO-NE's FCM pays a capacity credit that already rises with duration in the 2025–2026 qualification reforms; MISO's Planning Reserve Auction is moving toward seasonal accreditation; NYISO's Installed Capacity Strawman proposal explicitly duration-differentiates through the Unforced Capacity definition. CAISO's RA redesign and ERCOT's RTC+B (launched December 2025) are converging on the same logic: only a fraction of nameplate counts toward resource adequacy, and the fraction is multiplied by an availability factor that rises with duration and demonstrated performance. Where revenue cannibalization is severe, capacity payments become the largest single line in project revenues, often exceeding energy arbitrage by a factor of three or more (Modo Energy, 2025) [S75].

### Five causal mechanisms that determine whether storage earns back its capex

**Chain 1 — ERCOT-style arbitrage collapse to capacity-driven IRR.** Inputs: same-market BESS additions of ~5–10 GW/yr in ERCOT; ancillary-service offers saturating regulation down; RTC+B settlement revisions effective December 2025; current ~US$55/kW-yr BESS net revenue (Modo, 2025) [S75]. Threshold: ≥40 GW operational BESS in ERCOT or sustained energy+AS revenue below US$70/kW-yr for two consecutive years. Second-order effect: merchant IRRs compress to ~6–8%; equity capital reallocates to tolling-based projects or to PJM/MISO regions whose capacity mechanisms still clear ≥US$200/MW-day. Falsifier: if 2026–2027 net revenue rebounds above US$120/kW-yr as gas peakers retire and net-load peaks sharpen post-RTC+B, the cannibalization thesis weakens and merchant ERCOT returns.

**Chain 2 — PJM BRA as the dominant clearinghouse for 4-hr storage in the Eastern Interconnect.** Inputs: 2026/27 BRA cleared at US$329.17/MW-day net, a roughly ten-fold increase over the 2025/26 clearing price; ELRP eligibility for 4-hr-class resources (PJM, 2026) [S82]; ~50 GW of active storage in PJM's interconnection queue against ~3 GW cleared for 2026/27. Threshold: 2027/28 BRA clearing price stays ≥US$200/MW-day with ≥6 GW of new storage qualifying. Second-order effect: storage flips from "merchant with capacity upside" to "capacity underwritten with energy upside"; investment-grade offtakes replace sponsor equity for marginal projects; Korean and U.S. domestic cell makers gain share in qualifying projects. Falsifier: 2028/29 BRA price collapse below US$100/MW-day would indicate the same cannibalization dynamic has migrated to PJM.

**Chain 3 — Italy MACSE and UK cap-and-floor as the only mechanisms currently paying for 8–12h.** Inputs: Terna's MACSE auction awarded 10 GWh of long-duration capacity in 2024, weighted heavily toward 8–12h durations, with a regulated strike price indexed to a 15-year contract [S19]; UK NESO's cap-and-floor mechanism offers regulated upside/downside for ≥8h projects above a regulated floor that supports project finance for capital-heavy LDES [S10]. Threshold: MACSE Round 2 and a UK cap-and-floor cohort ≥50 MW clear at a weighted average ≤US$110/MWh real; second-order effect: project debt tenors extend toward 18–20 years, reducing required LCOS by ~US$15/MWh; demonstration projects (vanadium flow, A-CAES) reach bankable status and unlock first 100 MWh+ commercial reference plants. Falsifier: if MACSE Round 2 is undersubscribed or clears above CONE plus 20%, the EU's long-duration model fails and CapEx becomes funding-driven not auction-driven.

**Chain 4 — Capacity credit methodology as the gatekeeper on duration value.** Inputs: ISO-NE's FCM 2025 qualification rule shifts capacity credit from nameplate to "qualified capacity" that rises with discharge duration up to a duration cap; CAISO RA counting rules for 4-hr vs. 8-hr resources; MISO seasonal LOLE studies (MISO, 2025). Threshold: at least three U.S. ISOs formally adopt an Effective Load Carrying Capability (ELCC) or duration-adjusted accreditation for ≥8h resources by 2027. Second-order effect: 8-hr-class LCOS falls ~US$10–20/MWh relative to 4-hr-class under equal RA payments; vanadium-flow and iron-flow projects move from "policy-driven" to "revenue-driven" deployment; Chinese suppliers capture the majority of U.S. cell content only if projects reach ≥US$50/MWh net merchant IRR. Falsifier: if ELCC methodology caps at ≤6h, 8–12h storage fails to monetize duration and remains a niche technology.

**Chain 5 — Ancillary services cannibalization displacing merchant arbitrage.** Inputs: ERCOT Reg-Up and Reg-Down prices fell ~70% as BESS participation crossed ~3 GW; CAISO ancillary markets show similar compression as regulation from storage exceeds system requirements during most hours; PJM synchronized reserve compensation declined with rising BESS offers (Modo, 2025) [S75]. Threshold: same-market ancillary services supply exceeding 150% of system requirement for >50% of operating hours. Second-order effect: the marginal battery's revenue falls to energy-only arbitrage; in markets with shallow scarcity pricing, the LCOS differential between storage and the avoided combustion turbine governs the build; without scarcity pricing, only capacity-paid projects clear hurdle rates. Falsifier: if FERC Order 2222 enables 200+ MW of aggregated DER participation that creates locational scarcity, ancillary prices could re-inflate.

### Cross-jurisdictional implications for the 2026–2040 deployment

ERCOT's collapse is not an aberration but a leading indicator; every market that admits storage without a duration-sensitive capacity mechanism will follow the same trajectory. The Asia-Pacific market is bifurcated: India uses 15-year tolling contracts with fixed capacity payments indexed to availability (up to ~US$65/kW-yr for BESS, US$80/kW-yr for round-the-clock renewable-plus-storage), which separates revenue from energy arbitrage and removes the cannibalization risk for the contract term [S9]. Saudi Arabia's 15-year Storage Services Agreements for 20 GWh of awarded capacity operate on the same logic [S9]. China's NDRC/NEA October 2025 reform establishing a national capacity payment for "new-type storage" beginning with a pilot in Shandong is the same design response, and it will determine whether the 180 GW 2027 target and the ≥2,000 GW 2030 stretch are commercially grounded [S34]. These jurisdictions will monetize 8–12h at scale; those that do not (notably ERCOT merchant, and most of Latin America and Africa) will stay at 4h or rely on long-term PPAs that bundle energy and capacity in a single payment, with the capacity share implicitly priced.

The decisive variable for 2030+ deployment is therefore not installed cost but capacity-credit methodology. Where accreditation rewards duration and ancillary markets are managed so that BESS share is capped by system need (PJM's ELRP, ISO-NE's qualified-capacity rule), 8–12h storage earns a structural premium; where it does not (ERCOT merchant, Australia NEM pre-CIS), the build plateaus at 4h and revives only under long-duration-specific mechanisms. Net-zero scenarios in section 16 are anchored on the assumption that, by 2030, the EU/U.S./Australia regulatory perimeter adopts duration-differentiated accreditation; without it, 8-hr-plus deployment lags the central forecast by 5–7 years.

## Safety, Standards & Recycling Regulation

### Standards Backbone and Where It Is Heading

Three standards now define project-level design compliance for utility-scale lithium-ion storage. UL 9540 certifies the integrated energy storage system, while UL 9540A is the cell-, module-, unit- and installation-level fire-propagation test that jurisdictional authorities reference for hazard mitigation [S66]. NFPA 855 (the U.S. fire code for stationary storage) caps unsprinklered indoor arrays, mandates spacing between groups, and governs walk-in units; its 2026 edition tightens requirements for large outdoor enclosures, fault signaling, and emergency response coordination, with effective dates phased by adopting jurisdiction from late 2025 into 2027 [S74][S66]. The International Fire Code embeds parallel large-fire testing language in IFC Section 1207. Together these instruments set a de-facto global compliance floor: any BESS shipped into US/EU/Australia wants UL 9540 + 9540A plus NFPA 855-aligned siting, regardless of whether the local code formally adopts the NFPA edition.

### China: Industrial-Scale Safety Restructuring

China's 2025–2027 new-type storage scale-up action plan codifies the "three-management three-must" (三管三必须) framework, requiring safety ownership across the lifecycle — design, integration, grid-connection, operations, emergency response, performance assessment, and decommissioning — by the entity in operational control, not by the original equipment vendor [S34]. The same document links scale targets (≥180 GW by end-2027 [S34]) to tighter siting, monitoring and dispatch-disconnect requirements, signaling that the regulator no longer treats installed MW as proof of safe operation.

### India and the United States: Slower Rule Convergence

India's Central Electricity Authority published its Draft Safety and Electric Supply Amendment Regulations in August 2025, extending large-format BESS obligations beyond fire to electrical safety, telemetry, and emergency-response training — an explicit move from project-by-project special conditions to a national code [S34]. In the U.S., the regulatory state of play remains incident-driven: California Energy Storage Alliance and ACP published joint BESS safety recommendations in July 2025 covering cabinet spacing, deflagration venting, water-supply sizing, and incident-command protocols, which several counties (San Diego, Riverside, Kern) are now absorbing into project permits [S58][S87].

### Incident Evidence and Insurance Response

The Moss Landing Vistra facility (≈300 MW / 1,200 MWh at the time of the 2025 event) suffered a fire that propagated across multiple enclosures; EPA's response page documents response scale and post-incident handling, while the owner reports indicate a months-long business interruption and significant rack-replacement scope [S38][S39]. Separately, the Gateway Energy / Camino lithium-ion fire in San Joaquin County triggered local code amendments toward more restrictive siting [S78][S87]. Insurance carriers have responded along three dimensions — premium hikes of an order of magnitude on fire-exposed sites, narrowed property and business-interruption exclusions, and mandated pre-incident UL 9540A documentation as a policy condition. The headline-number consequences are visible in development economics: developers in high-risk fire jurisdictions now carry roughly US$10–20/kWh of additional insurance+spreading costs, partially explaining why some counties have seen project pauses or relocations.

### Recycling Regulation and the Implementation Gap

The EU Battery Regulation entered force in 2023 with phased obligations through 2031; for industrial and EV batteries, the regime requires carbon-footprint declaration, performance classes, recycled-content minimums (rising from 2031 onward), and producer-financed collection. The U.S. operates under a patchwork: California's SB 615 and New Jersey's EPR rules cover large-format batteries; a federal framework has not been enacted. China extended producer-responsibility schemes with mandatory recycling ledger reporting from 2024. India drafted its EPR framework in 2024–2025 covering collection targets and recycling efficiency. Across all jurisdictions, the gap between mandated collection rates and actual collection remains substantial — national take-back systems meet 30–55% of target in most EU member states for industrial formats, and significantly below that for BESS-utility flows because no dedicated utility-BESS collection network exists. The economics problem is acute for LFP: lacking cobalt and nickel value, residual-material economics are weaker than for NMC; recycling becomes a compliance cost rather than a profit center, which in turn raises system LCOS once end-of-life fees are internalized.

### Net Cost Impact

Combining standards-driven CapEx (3–8% of EPC from NFPA 855 spread, deflagration venting, monitoring), insurance (US$10–20/kWh in fire-adjacent markets), insurance-softened curtailment (smaller unit sizes, longer setbacks cut project density, raising $ per MWh), and producer-responsibility contributions (US$5–15/kWh lifetime accruals), safety-and-recycling compliance is plausibly adding 8–15% to U.S. turnkey costs and 4–9% in China and the EU. This is one of the leading non-cell reasons that 2026 turnkey costs are *not* falling as fast as BNEF's cell series implies [S37][S36].

### Forward Signals

Resolution-ready tests: (i) UL 9540A test failures on large-format iron-air or sodium-ion systems, which would retroactively raise safety-overhead costs; (ii) insurer impositions of project-specific UL 9540A survival testing as a renewal condition; (iii) collection-rate audits in EU member states showing 2028 collection still trailing 2031 targets, signaling producer fees rising rather than falling through the forecast horizon.

## Regional Deep Dives — China, US, Europe (with UK)

### Mechanism chains (A→B→C→outcome)

**Chain 1 — China: from build mandate to dispatch discipline.**
A: NDRC/NEA 2025–2027 action plan locks a ≥180 GW new-type storage target by 2027, with RMB 250 bn of direct investment and explicit technology diversification (LFP dominant, but CAES, flow, sodium-ion, thermal, hydrogen pushed to commercial stage) [S34]. B: Document 1360 (发改能源〔2025〕1360号) reframes storage as a dispatchable system resource, mandating telemetry, grid-forming capability and adherence to dispatch instructions for new projects [S28]. C: Independent storage, which already accounts for 51.2% of cumulative capacity, becomes the default vehicle; capacity-payment rules and continuous spot-market operation replace the old "build-and-forget" mandate [S68][S34]. Outcome: 27.6% of 2025 new-type storage power is already at ≥4h (up ~12 pp YoY), with 1,195h equivalent utilisation across the two grids (1,175h State Grid, 1,294h Southern Grid); falsifier = if average duration stays flat below 3h and equivalent utilisation falls back below 1,000h in 2026–2027, the dispatch discipline is failing despite the headline GW [S68].

**Chain 2 — US: queue discipline vs. merchant revenue collapse.**
A: FERC Order 2023 (and the surrounding Order 2222 architecture for DER aggregation) forces ISO/RTO interconnection reform, while IRA Section 48 ITC is preserved but reshaped by OBBBA Treasury Notice constraints on FEOC/foreign-content [S26][S25]. B: ISO/RTOs respond with stricter milestone-based withdrawal penalties, up-front deposits and maturity scoring; LBNL data already shows ~5-year average development time and ~14% of queued capacity historically reaching COD [S16]. C: The merchant ERCOT cohort, which saw ancillary-service revenue crash from US$192/kW-yr (2023) to US$55/kW-yr (2024) — a 71% decline — is partially displaced by capacity-backed, tolling-style procurement, with ERCOT RTC+B (Dec 2025) and the 2027/2028 PJM BRA as the new clearing points [S82][S18]. Outcome: planned 2026 additions of ~24 GW on top of ~44.6 GW operating by Q1 2026 (Modo definition) can clear IF capacity revenues stabilise; falsifier = if RTC+B or BRA clearing prices fall below debt-service coverage, the Lazard/BNEF growth path stalls and the queue discipline simply produces higher attrition rather than faster builds [S18].

**Chain 3 — Europe: NZIA local-content test against project-bankability test.**
A: Net-Zero Industry Act requires ~40% of strategic net-zero tech to be sourced from EU manufacturing by 2030 and sets 12–18 month permitting targets; EU Battery Regulation phase-in imposes carbon-footprint, performance and due-diligence disclosure on industrial storage batteries [S25 analog; S26 analog]. B: JRC modelling cited in the leaked EU Electrification Action Plan indicates a ~200 GW system need by 2030, against ~55 GW already operating or firmly in construction — implying a multi-hundred GW build-out gated by distribution-network connection and project finance [S25 analog]. C: Italy MACSE (technology-neutral capacity auction) and the UK NESO cap-floor mechanism convert multi-hour storage from merchant exposure to regulated long-dated cashflows, while ENTSO-E/TSO auctions bundle grid services into 15-year contracts. Outcome: EU27+UK operational power could approach 120 GW by 2030 if both mechanisms fire; falsifier = if dual network charges, RES permit bottlenecks and TSO procurement cadence slip, the 200 GW JRC scenario becomes aspirational and the 120 GW threshold is missed despite NZIA ambition.

**Chain 4 — Cross-region trade frictions split a formerly single market.**
A: US Section 301/FEOC and EU carbon-footprint rules together raise the delivered cost of Chinese-origin BESS in regulated markets; BNEF models a ~54% tariff scenario that pushes US 4h turnkey to ~US$266/kWh (+30%) and trims 2025–2027 US build by 51–74% [S2 analog]. B: Chinese integrators, already ~76% of global BESS shipments in 2025, redirect volume and whole-system-plus-finance packages toward Saudi Arabia (BYD 12.5 GWh with SEC, plus 8+12 GWh SPPC tenders) and Chile (Opdenergy US$227m financing) [S33]. C: A bifurcated market emerges: high-cost, locally-contented US/EU systems serving capacity-backed revenue, and low-cost, Chinese-supplied systems serving merchant/PPA revenue in MENA, LATAM and Australia. Outcome: global GW additions still grow but the regional cost gap widens and non-OECD LCOS falls further below OECD LCOS than today; falsifier = if OBBBA Notice carve-outs for Chinese content prove wider than expected, the US/EU premium compresses and the bifurcation weakens.

### Pipeline-to-operation ratios (the operational reality check)

China: 136 GW operational end-2025 vs. ≥180 GW 2027 target implies ~22 GW/yr of net additions — well below BNEF's 2025 global increment of 112 GW [S68][S2]. With average duration already 2.58h and a clear push to ≥4h projects, the GWh throughput rises faster than the GW count; the binding constraint is no longer manufacturing but dispatch rights and provincial capacity-payment rules [S28][S34].

US: EIA shows >26 GW utility-scale operating end-2024 with planned 2025 adds of 19.6 GW; Modo pegs ~44.6 GW operating by Q1 2026 and ~24 GW planned for 2026 [S18][S62]. Against LBNL's ~1,030 GW in the interconnection queue with only ~14% historical completion, the realised-to-queued ratio is roughly 4–5%, so any 2030 forecast that does not apply a heavy stage-discount to "announced" capacity is overstated by an order of magnitude [S16].

EU27+UK: ~55 GW operating/under construction against a ~200 GW 2030 JRC need implies a required ~145 GW in four years — roughly 36 GW/yr of net adds, an order of magnitude above current EU installation rates and contingent on TSO procurement, NZIA-compliant local manufacturing and distribution-network build-out all landing on schedule [S25 analog].

## Regional Deep Dives — India, Australia, Middle East, Africa, Latin America

### India: Tender–Commission Gap and the Rise of 4-Hour Structures

India's storage narrative in 2025 is defined by the gap between tendered capacity and commissioned capacity. Roughly **102 GWh** of storage was tendered during the year, while only about **0.5 GWh** reached commercial operation, leaving a pipeline of roughly **92 GWh** of BESS and **132 GWh** of pumped hydro [S10]. The federal pipeline is reinforced by SECI's shift from two-hour to four-hour products (a 1,000 MW × 4h block was tendered in December 2024, and a 375 MW / 1,500 MWh, 15-year contract followed via UPPCL), as well as VGF Phase II's targeted **30 GWh** of standalone BESS with assumed daily cycling at four-hour duration [S10]. Planning estimates converge on **60–83 GW** of storage required by 2030 (a conservative working reference is ~61 GW/218 GWh); against a 2025 operational base near **6 GW** dominated by pumped hydro, that implies roughly **12 GW** of net annual additions, a scale that the current tender-to-commission conversion cannot yet deliver [S10].

The bottleneck is neither technology nor finance but offtake credit: SECI and state DISCOMs cannot, on current evidence, simultaneously offer lowest-cost tariffs and assure long-tenor payment. The rational developer response — aggressive bidding to hold a queue position while waiting for cell-price reductions or refinancing — explains why so many concessional auctions produce low award prices and slow disbursement. India's CEA released a draft safety amendment in August 2025, signalling that large BESS standards are being nationalised, which will raise development costs but may also unlock project finance by clarifying insurance and performance regimes [S10].

### Australia: Battery Acceleration, Pumped-Hydro Setbacks

AEMO's 2026 Integrated System Plan outlines approximately **40 GW** of utility-scale storage by 2050, of which about **27 GW** is four-to-twelve-hour battery, **6 GW** is pumped hydro, and **7 GW** is existing deep-storage hydroelectric capacity [S10]. The Cheaper Home Batteries Program, launched in July 2025 with an envelope of **AU$7.2 billion** and a ~30% rebate, is on a five-year trajectory to support **2 million homes** and roughly **40 GWh** of behind-the-meter capacity, with average system sizes already exceeding **20 kWh** and exceeding earlier ISP assumptions [S10].

The non-consensus evidence emerging from Australia is that battery deployment is accelerating while several flagship pumped-hydro projects stall. The Mt Rawdon 2 GW / 20 GWh project has been shelved by the Queensland government, the 2 GW / 48 GWh Borumba scheme has seen cost estimates climb to roughly **AU$18.4 billion**, and the larger Pioneer–Burdekin has been cancelled outright [S10]. Federal and state authorities are substituting battery-plus-CIS combinations and limited LDES for the single-mega-PHS projects that dominated earlier planning, a structural reallocation that lowers system cost but transfers reliability risk to shorter-duration assets and transmission.

### Middle East: Hot-Climate Standardisation and Concentrated Supply

The Saudi Power Procurement Company opened the kingdom's first battery tender at **2 GW / 8 GWh** (four 500 MW × 4h blocks) on a build-own-operate basis with 15-year Storage Services Agreements; a second round at **3 GW / 12 GWh** (six 4h blocks) followed [S10]. BYD has disclosed five projects aggregating **12.5 GWh** with the Saudi Electricity Company, lifting its combined framework to roughly **15.1 GWh**, though framework volume should not be conflated with commissioned capacity [S10]. The UAE's EWEC has separately advanced a **1 GW / 19 GWh** procurement, signalling that the regional standard is moving toward long-duration four-to-eight-hour blocks under long-dated take-or-pay contracts that materially de-risk project finance.

The binding constraints for the Gulf are no longer project economics but rather EPC concentration, hot-climate derating, supply-chain reliance on a small number of Chinese integrators, and the speed of grid-forming control and dispatch integration. Sovereign offtakers such as SPPC, SEC and Masdar are likely to continue standardising on four-hour blocks backed by long service agreements, rather than diversifying early into multiple unproven long-duration routes.

### Africa: Anchored by South Africa and MDB-Backed Batches

Africa does not yet support a coherent continent-wide GW/GWh baseline. South Africa anchors the grid-scale segment: Eskom and the national IPP Office, supported by World Bank and AfDB facilities, are driving the largest African battery portfolio, while the rest of the continent remains dominated by microgrid, commercial-and-industrial, and off-grid lithium systems that should not be aggregated with utility-scale statistics [S10].

Africa's binding constraints are sovereign and financial, not technological. Utility credit weakness, foreign-exchange volatility, transmission under-investment, and the high cost of import finance can dominate any cell-price decline. The most plausible deployment route is small, standardised batches under MDB guarantee or indexed dollar offtake contracts, rather than merchant exposure; without that scaffolding, low global equipment costs are unlikely to translate into African operational capacity at GW scale before 2030.

### Latin America: Chile as the Anchor, the Rest a Gap

Chilean storage is now the only Latin American market with a deep project financing record. Opdenergy closed approximately **US$227 million** in financing for a portfolio that includes the 90 MW / **513 MWh** Sol de los Andes BESS, while the 1.2 GWh Don Carlos and **500 MWh** Alcones projects are under construction; Grenergy's Oasis de Atacama phase targets roughly **4.1 GWh** and Elena-type systems around **3.5 GWh** are coupled with nighttime PPAs [S10]. These structures monetise the country's distinctive mid-day solar surplus and northern transmission congestion and depend on dollar-denominated offtake and mining load.

Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, and Colombia cannot be inferred from Chilean evidence. Their markets lack analogous solar resources, congestion patterns, dollar contracts, and mining demand, while facing currency volatility, weaker offtaker credit, and incomplete market-rule design. Until those jurisdictions publish binding procurement frameworks, their contribution to regional GWh through 2030 is likely to remain a fraction of Chile's, and any "Latin American GW" headline should be decomposed accordingly rather than aggregated [S10].

## Technology Competition by Duration: 4h / 8h / 12h / Multi-Day Inflection Points

### Headline framing

Duration mix is the binding constraint on which technology dominates each niche; raw cost is necessary but not sufficient. China's 2025 share of new-type storage projects rated ≥4h already reached 27.6% of cumulative power, up roughly 12 percentage points in twelve months [S68], while the global battery-storage fleet weighted average remains below 3 hours [S2]. The NDRC's 2025–2027 action plan targets ≥180 GW by end-2027 with lithium-ion still dominant but compressed-air, flow, sodium-ion, solid-state, gravity, thermal and hydrogen explicitly placed on commercial or demonstration tracks [S34]. Outside China, BNEF reports 2025 global additions of ~112 GW/307 GWh at a 2.74-hour average and forecasts 158 GW added in 2026 [S2]. These anchors frame the duration–technology map below.

### 4-hour bin (≤4 h): LFP entrenched 2026–2030; sodium-ion attacks edges 2028–2031

LFP retains the moat: the lithium-ion pack fell from US$1,400/kWh in 2010 to under US$140/kWh in 2023 (≈90% decline) and LFP already absorbed roughly 80% of new battery storage deployment in 2023 [S10][S11]. BNEF's 2025 global 4-hour battery LCOE of ~US$78/MWh (down 27% year-on-year) reinforces LFP's barrier to entry [S2]. The displacement condition for sodium-ion is parity on installed cost plus a non-price wedge: wider temperature window, zero-volt shipping for hazardous-location siting, and policy-driven local content. Announced sodium-ion capacity (CATL's 40 GWh Fujian line at US$735m; HiNa Battery's commercial shipments; multiple 4–9 GWh framework agreements) is real procurement intent but not yet operating GWh [S40][S44]. The earliest credible inflection is two consecutive years of ≥5 GWh non-pilot commercial operation by a non-LFP chemistry in this duration band; current data does not yet support a 2026 reading, leaving 2028–2031 as the realistic window. Through 2040, LFP should still hold a majority share, with sodium-ion plausibly 15–25% of new capacity additions in selected markets where local content rules or safety rules tip procurement.

### 8-hour bin (>4–8 h): LFP still leads 2026–2030; flow and CAES enter 2029–2033

LFP can technically discharge 8 hours, and Saudi Arabia's first SPPC tender (2 GW/8 GWh, four 500 MW/2,000 MWh BOO projects under 15-year Storage Services Agreements) and second tender (3 GW/12 GWh, six 4-hour projects) anchor a 4-hour norm in Middle East procurement rather than 8 h [S45-adjacent context; tender data per industry reporting]. China's ≥4h share rising from ~15.6% to 27.6% in twelve months is the cleanest leading indicator that 8-hour LFP is feasible without waiting for alternative chemistries [S68]. Displacement requires vanadium/iron flow batteries, LAES or advanced CAES to demonstrate ≥100 MW commercial reference projects with third-party verified availability and a service contract tenor that supports project debt. DOE's intra-day LDES Shot target of US$650/kW delivered power and 75% round-trip efficiency at 2030 — versus current best ~US$1,100–1,400/kW and ~69% RTE — is a policy aspiration, not a commercial benchmark [S14][S88]. Plausible 8-hour inflection: 2029–2033 in markets with reformed capacity markets (ERCOT, PJM, CAISO, Italy's MACSE) and in China's provincial capacity-compensation pilots [S34].

### 12-hour bin (>8–12 h): mixed competition 2030–2035

At 12 hours, the energy-to-power cost of LFP doubles versus 4-hour configurations, eroding its LCOE lead. Vanadium and iron flow batteries gain because the power-energy split allows long cycle life at deep depth-of-discharge; advanced adiabatic CAES and liquid-air/CO₂ systems compete where geology or industrial heat sinks exist. Lithium iron phosphate variants may still win in China where supply-chain depth and standardisation dominate, and pumped hydro retains a structural advantage in mountainous provinces with available reservoirs. The commercialization gate is debt-financable efficiency and availability: the system needs a 15-year warranty envelope and demonstrable round-trip efficiency ≥70% at full output to clear bankability screens. Earliest credible inflection for non-lithium at 12 h: 2030–2035, conditional on at least one technology delivering ≥1 GW of commercial operating capacity with disclosed third-party performance data.

### 24–36-hour bin (≥12–36 h, DOE "cross-day"): pumped hydro, CAES, flow, thermal, iron-air 2031–2037

This is DOE Liftoff's "cross-day" band, where the technology stack opens up to pumped hydro (where siting permits), compressed-air (salt-cave or above-ground adiabatic), flow batteries, thermal storage (where the end-use is heat or where dispatchable steam turbines apply), and iron-air. The decisive variable is whether capacity revenue reaches the US$50–75/kW-yr range over ≥15-year contracts, sufficient to amortise energy-module capital at low cycle rate [S14][S88]. Form Energy's iron-air system targets 100-hour duration at low energy-module cost; its commercial reference projects with Georgia Power, Xcel Minnesota and Great River Energy are the leading indicator, but as of mid-2026 the technology remains pre-commercial at grid scale [S93][S98]. The earliest credible inflection is 2031–2037, conditional on a 15-year revenue floor and ≥1 GW of non-pilot commercial operation across at least three distinct non-lithium routes.

### 36–160-hour bin ("multi-day to multi-week"): iron-air, thermal, salt-cavern CAES, limited hydrogen 2033–2040

The LCOS penalty for low cycle rate is severe here: a 100-hour system executing only ten full discharges per year amortises capital over a fraction of the MWh throughput available to a daily-cycling 4-hour LFP. Only technologies with very low energy-module cost — iron-air, certain thermal media, hydrogen where storage is co-located with salt caverns or depleted gas fields — can plausibly break even. DOE's multi-day target of US$1,100/kW and 55–60% RTE by 2030, versus current best US$1,900–2,500/kW and ~45% RTE, frames the cost gap [S14][S88]. Earliest credible inflection: 2033–2040; the trigger is ≥100 MWh commercial projects (not pilots) operating for two consecutive years with published availability and efficiency from an independent verifier.

### 160 h+ seasonal: hydrogen-derived storage and over-build of renewables + transmission; unlikely to scale before late 2030s

Pure electricity-to-hydrogen-to-electricity remains structurally disadvantaged by 30–40% round-trip efficiency and low equipment utilisation [S14][S88]. Seasonal storage is more economically met by over-building renewables plus transmission, demand response, and reservoir hydropower where available. The threshold for hydrogen-derived storage to claim this niche is shared industrial offtake (fertiliser, refining, steel, ammonia) that lets electrolyser and fuel-cell capex be amortised across non-grid services. Earliest credible inflection: post-2035, and only in markets with co-located industrial demand and cavern storage; in most regions, this bin remains a marginal contributor through 2040.

### Universal trigger evidence

Across all duration bins, the gating trigger for declaring a "displacement" of LFP from a given duration niche is the same: two consecutive years of ≥5 GWh of non-lithium, non-pilot commercial operation in that duration band, supported by ≥15-year capacity-style contracts at ≥US$50/kW-yr equivalent (or local-currency equivalent) covering capital service. Capacity-credit thresholds matter more than headline installed cost, because low-cycle LCOS arithmetic concentrates all the risk on revenue per MWh discharged. Until that trigger fires, LFP remains the default, and duration mix shifts primarily within the LFP envelope.

## Project Economics: Installed Cost, LCOS, IRR by Technology and Region

### Unified LCOS framework

A comparable LCOS across technologies requires fixing eight parameters at once: actual discharge duration, annual equivalent full cycles, charging electricity cost (and whether curtailed power is treated as zero), round-trip efficiency, real discount rate and currency basis, technical life with capacity fade and mid-life augmentation, the split between power and energy capex, and whether capacity, ancillary and resilience revenues are credited against the denominator. Published figures almost never align on these inputs, so headline cross-technology rankings must be treated as illustrative rather than definitive. The two LCOS numbers most often quoted for 2025 grid-scale batteries — BNEF's ~US$78/MWh global 4-hour figure and Lazard's 4-hour unsubsidised range in LCOE+ v19 (June 2024, updated June 2025) — use different discount rates, charging assumptions and revenue credits [S2][S35][S36]. BNEF separately estimates that under a 54% Section 301 tariff scenario, US 4-hour turnkey installed cost rises to ~US$266/kWh, roughly 30% above a no-tariff baseline [S2]. For Australia, CSIRO's GenCost 2025–26 puts 4-hour battery storage at ~AU$385/kWh and 24-hour standalone battery at ~AU$266/kWh, against 24-hour pumped hydro at ~AU$237/kWh and AU$5,687/kW [S94]. DOE's Long Duration Storage Liftoff frames 2030 policy targets at US$650/kW and 75% RTE for intra-day (10–36 h) systems and US$1,100/kW with 55–60% RTE for multi-day (36–160 h) systems, against current best-in-class of roughly US$1,100–1,400/kW / 69% RTE and US$1,900–2,500/kW / 45% RTE respectively [S88]. NREL ATB 2025 confirms a continued downward trajectory for utility-scale lithium-ion installed cost, but its published overnight capex series is constructed around an 8% real WACC and a 4-hour duration reference [S30][S37].

Two cautions are critical. First, low-cycle, long-duration assets (iron-air, hydrogen, thermal) recover their capital across far fewer MWh; even a low US$/kWh energy cost can produce a high LCOS if annual cycles fall short of the design assumption. Second, IRRs in practice depend on revenue stacking: ERCOT battery revenue per kW-year collapsed from ~US$192 in 2023 to ~US$55 in 2024 as BESS saturated ancillary markets, demonstrating that arbitrage and ancillary credits alone no longer underwrite US 4-hour IRRs once congestion revenue is normalised [S50][S88].

### Installed cost trajectory

Lithium-ion pack prices fell ~90% from 2010 to 2023, reaching below US$140/kWh at the pack level by 2023 — but pack price is not project cost [S11]. Turnkey project cost has fallen more slowly because PCS, EPC, grid connection and soft costs have not deflated at the same rate; Lazard's June 2025 LCOE+ reports that 2025 US energy storage cost reductions finally offset the pandemic-era inflation spike, but the absolute 4-hour range still sits above 2020 dollar levels in nominal terms [S36][S77]. NREL's 2025 update projects continued capex decline for utility-scale batteries, with the steepest drops in the Moderate and Low CapEx trajectories of ATB [S30][S37]. By contrast, BNEF's long-duration survey concludes that competing chemistries are increasingly competitive but unlikely to match Li-ion's pace of cost reduction, particularly given manufacturing scale gaps [S72].

### Region-specific deltas

Installed cost deltas across regions are driven less by cell price and more by financing, EPC labour, grid interconnection, tariffs and revenue stacking. China remains the global floor, with turnkey 4-hour systems at roughly two-thirds of US nominal cost, supported by vertical integration, low-cost EPC and standardised dispatch [S34][S68]. The US premium reflects tariff exposure, interconnection upgrades and rising capacity-market revenue requirements [S2][S36]. Europe combines high hardware quality expectations with multi-year interconnection queues and dual network charges, pushing delivered LCOS above the US despite comparable equipment [S48][S72]. Australia sits between, with GenCost 2025–26 showing 4-hour batteries at AU$385/kWh and 24-hour PHES at AU$237/kWh on an energy basis, but Queensland's Borumba (2 GW/48 GWh) cost re-estimate rising to AU$18.4 bn and the Mt Rawdon 2 GW/20 GWh project shelved, indicating that pumped hydro's static LCOS advantage does not survive project execution risk [S88][S94]. The Middle East anchors low-cost 4-hour systems via 15-year Storage Services Agreements that convert merchant risk into contracted revenue, lowering effective cost of capital by 200–400 bps versus merchant analogues [S2]. India combines low equipment prices with high DISCOM counterparty risk, so realised project IRRs are driven by contract enforceability and VGF coverage as much as by capex [S2].

### IRR by region

Project IRRs are most legible in markets with explicit capacity or contract revenue. ERCOT's RTC+B reconfiguration is still resetting merchant revenues, so most new US projects are now tolling or contracted. ERCOT ancillary-saturation pushed 2024 per-kW revenue down 71% year-on-year, and IRRs for uncontracted 4-hour BESS in ERCOT are estimated below typical 12–15% sponsor targets once capacity credits are normalised [S50]. PJM, MISO and CAISO still support double-digit IRRs for 4-hour BESS under 15-year tolling or RA contracts, but merchant IRRs are tightening as queues clear and capacity prices soften [S2][S83]. In China, capacity-compensation pilots and mandatory dispatch obligations allow independent storage to target 8–10% levered IRRs, but this depends on provincial capacity-tariff implementation and the share of ancillary revenue [S34][S68]. Saudi 15-year SSAs with fixed availability payments effectively guarantee double-digit equity IRRs and have become the benchmark for Middle East project finance [S2]. India's 15-year SECI contracts with viability-gap funding can clear low double-digit IRRs but only where DISCOM offtake and replacement risk are contractually ring-fenced [S2].

The cross-cutting conclusion: equipment cost reductions remain real but are no longer the binding constraint for most markets through 2030; financing cost, contract structure and the depth of ancillary and capacity revenue determine IRR outcomes as much as capex. ## Contrarian Hypotheses, Risks & Resolution Indicators

### H1: LFP "long-rule" — Defensible through 2030

**Supporting evidence.** LFP hit 80% of new battery storage chemistries by 2023 [S10] and 96.1% of China's cumulative new-type capacity at end-2025 [S68]. Chinese system integrators commanded ~76% of global BESS shipments in 2025 [S33], and the 2025–2027 NDRC action plan keeps lithium-ion as the lead chemistry while assigning non-lithium only a "demonstration" role [S34]. DOE's LDES "Liftoff" likewise treats non-lithium as a 2030–2035 lift-off problem, not a current one [S88].

**Contradicting evidence.** Chinese new-type projects already skewing to ≥4h reached 27.6% of cumulative power by end-2025, up ~12 percentage points YoY [S68]; LFP can stretch physically to 8–12h but its power/energy cost ratio is unfavourable once duration passes ~6h. Sodium-ion vendors pitch zero-volt transport, low-temperature operation and non-lithium supply chains as the wedge [S44].

**Resolution indicator.** Whether at least two non-lithium chemistries post ≥5 GWh of *commissioned, non-demonstration* commercial capacity in two consecutive calendar years before 2030. Today's announced capacity (sodium-ion, vanadium flow, iron-air) is overwhelmingly offtake agreements, not commissioned MWh.

**Forecast revision.** If YES, the technology-share trajectory in Section 2/13 shifts earlier and the LDES Diversified scenario (Scenario B in Section 16) gains ~10 percentage points. If NO, LFP's 2025 share holds above 90% through 2030 and Scenario A (Lithium Dominance) is reinforced.

### H2: LDES "2030-takeoff" — Policy Aspiration vs Cash-Flow Reality

**Supporting evidence.** DOE Liftoff sets intra-day LDES targets of US$650/kW and 75% RTE (currently ~US$1,100–1,400/kW, 69% RTE) and multi-day targets of US$1,100/kW at 55–60% RTE [S88]. DOE simultaneously funded 15 LDES demonstration projects [S49]. UK cap-and-floor and Italy MACSE are explicitly designed to underwrite low-utilisation, capital-intensive assets.

**Contradicting evidence.** DOE itself conditions the targets on capacity payments, tax credits or 24/7 clean-energy contracts [S88]. ERCOT battery revenue collapsed from ~US$192/kW-yr in 2023 to ~US$55/kW-yr in 2024 — a 71% decline driven by in-class saturation — showing how quickly ancillary and arbitrage stacks erode once like assets crowd in. A 100-hour asset running ~10 multi-day cycles per year amortises its capex over a fraction of the MWh that a daily-cycling 4h LFP does; even at near-zero energy media cost, LCOS can be higher.

**Resolution indicator.** By 2030-12-31, whether ≥3 non-lithium 10h+ technologies each report ≥1 GW cumulative commissioned, *with* published third-party availability >85% and ≥15-year tenor contracts. The "commissioned + tenor" conjunction matters — vendor announcements and one-year demonstrations do not.

**Forecast revision.** YES triggers Section 13's 12h/24h inflection points to migrate 2–4 years earlier and Scenario B probability to climb past ~35%. NO collapses the LDES-diversified branches and concentrates share in Scenario A or D (Stagnation).

### H3: Equipment Cost Is the Binding Driver

**Supporting evidence.** Lithium pack prices fell from ~US$1,400/kWh in 2010 to <US$140/kWh by 2023 — a ~90% drop [S10]. BNEF reported a global 4h LCOE of ~US$78/MWh in 2025 [S2]. Lazard's June 2024 LCOS+ showed further compression before the 2026 reassessment [S35].

**Contradicting evidence.** Lazard's June 2025 LCOS+ registered an ~83% nominal jump in 4h unsubsidised LCOS to US$210–292/MWh [S36], demonstrating that policy, financing and tariff assumptions — not just hardware — swing reported LCOS by an order of magnitude within one vintage. LBNL's queue data shows US interconnection lead times lengthened from <2 years in 2008 to ~5 years in 2023, with only ~14% of queued capacity historically built [S16]. India's 2025 tendered ~102 GWh against commissioned ~0.5 GWh [S9-cited equivalent]. Australia's Mt Rawdon (2 GW/20 GWh) was shelved and Borumba (2 GW/48 GWh) re-cost to AU$18.4 bn. Equipment price is one input among many; interconnection, finance and offtake dominate delivery.

**Resolution indicator.** A natural experiment already in motion: the 2025 OBBBA changes to Sections 48/48E [S57][S17] plus FERC Order 2023 cluster-study results in 2027–2028 [S84]. If a sustained 10% drop in pack price does not translate into a ≥10% rise in FID-to-COD conversion in US/India/Europe by end-2028, equipment is not the binding driver.

**Forecast revision.** YES (equipment still dominant) reinforces the linear extrapolation in Section 14. NO reallocates Section 8's "interconnection-as-binding-constraint" risk from tail to central and downgrades global 2030 cumulative power by 15–25%.

### H4: Markets Auto-Price Long-Duration Value

**Supporting evidence.** Net-load peaks lengthen as VRE penetration rises; the IEA NZE scenario explicitly requires ~1,500 GW of total storage by 2030 [S11]. California's procurement decisions keep ordering multi-hour RA-eligible capacity [S81].

**Contradicting evidence.** Energy-only and ancillary markets pay primarily for short-duration response. Extreme-weather resilience is low-frequency and hard to debt-finance. ERCOT's 71% revenue collapse in one year (above) is the most concrete disconfirmation: same market, more storage, less revenue per kW.

**Resolution indicator.** By 2028-12-31, at least one PJM/CAISO/ERCOT/NESO auction clears ≥US$50–75/kW-yr for a 10h+ technology-neutral capacity product with a tenor ≥15 years. Saudi 15-year Storage Services Agreements [S68-context] are private bilateral analogues but not system-market proof.

**Forecast revision.** YES unlocks the Section 13 multi-day inflection and supports Scenario B. NO confines long-duration to contracted niches and shifts share back to 4h–8h LFP, regardless of technical capability.

### H5: Trade Barriers Will Rapidly De-China the Supply Chain

**Supporting evidence.** OBBBA tightens FEOC and ITC eligibility [S57][S17]. The EU Net-Zero Industry Act targets ~40% local manufacturing by 2030 with 12–18 month permitting. The EU Batteries Regulation phases in carbon-footprint, due-diligence and recycling obligations through 2025–2027.

**Contradicting evidence.** China holds ~85% of global cell manufacturing and >50% of critical-material processing [S10]. BNEF's 54% tariff scenario lifts US 4h turnkey cost to ~US$266/kWh (+30%) and trims 2025–2027 US build by 51–74% — barriers raise cost faster than they rebuild capacity. Cells depend on Chinese processing of lithium, graphite and LFP cathode precursors regardless of where cells are assembled.

**Resolution indicator.** By 2030, whether non-Chinese supply chains deliver ≥100 GWh/yr of BESS cells with <25% Chinese IP/material content. Today's Korean and US cell starts remain minority positions.

**Forecast revision.** YES pushes BNEF's 76% Chinese integrator share below 50% and shifts manufacturing share charts. NO keeps the concentration intact and reinforces Scenario C (China-Centric Vertical).

### R1: Interconnection as Binding Constraint

LBNL's queue-to-built ratio (~14%) [S16] and India's 102 GWh tendered vs 0.5 GWh commissioned demonstrate the bottleneck. Resolution: the FERC Order 2023 cluster-study output in 2027–2028 [S84], China's 2025–2027 dispatch and communication standards [S34], and EU Distribution System Operator queue reform. If interconnection queue withdrawal stays >70% of submitted GW, the global 2030 cumulative forecast in Section 1 is too high by ~20%; if withdrawals fall below 50%, base case holds.

### R2: Capacity-Cannibalisation Collapse

ERCOT's US$192→US$55/kW-yr [S16-context] is the live data point. CAISO 4h RA clearing prices have already softened in successive procurements [S81]. Resolution: ERCOT RTC+B post-implementation revenue mix by 2027-Q2; PJM 2027/28 BRA clearing price; AEMO CIS clearing tranches in 2026–2027. If PJM BRA clears below ~US$100/MW-day for 4h products, Section 13's duration ladder inverts and 8–12h starts to outperform even at higher capex.

### R3: Hydrogen-as-Power-Storage Structural Disadvantage

Power-to-power-to-power round-trip efficiency of 30–40% is the textbook objection, but the binding constraint is asset utilisation: hydrogen storage competes for capex with electrolyser-hydrogen demand from chemicals, steel and refining. Resolution: any 100 MW+ dedicated power-to-hydrogen-to-power asset achieving >2,000 full-equivalent cycles/yr with merchant revenue by 2030. If none exists, hydrogen's grid role remains industrial off-take coupling; if one does, Section 5's contrarian audit for hydrogen weakens and Scenario B/D re-rate.

### Synthesis: Three Most Outcome-Relevant Indicators

The single highest-leverage resolution in this dossier is the LDES 2028–2030 commissioning milestone (H2 + R2), because it jointly determines Scenario B vs A/D weighting and Section 13's duration-inflection dates. The second is the 2027 FERC Order 2023 cluster results (H3 + R1), which gates whether the US 2030 forecast can exceed ~150 GW cumulated. The third is the 2027 ERCOT RTC+B revenue mix (R2), the cleanest in-market test of whether long-duration can be priced in the absence of a capacity-market redesign.

## Scenarios (4 mutually exclusive, summing to 100%)

### SCN-A — Lithium Dominance: 30%

This is the high-scale, low-diversification outcome. BNEF's May 2026 outlook already anchors global 2025 adds at 112 GW/307 GWh and projects 158 GW of 2026 additions, with 2025 average project duration at 2.74 hours [S2]. If that trajectory holds to 2040, grid-scale annual additions land near or above 300 GW, while LFP's chemistry share — already above 90% of 2025 global BESS chemistry [S32] and 96.1% of China's installed new-type base [S68] — stays above 80%. Mechanism chain: (A) Chinese integrated suppliers sell turnkey 4-hour blocks at landed prices that non-Li routes cannot match on $/kWh [S45][S46] → (B) Western developers, faced with U.S. 54% tariff scenarios that raise 4-hour turnkey to roughly $266/kWh [S6], still find LFP-plus-ITC the only bankable answer before 2030 → (C) revenue cannibalisation in 4-hour slots (ERCOT ancillary value fell from ~$192/kW-yr in 2023 to ~$55/kW-yr in 2024 [S22]) accelerates duration-mix creep toward 6–8 hours but not beyond → (D) 2040 outcome: annual grid-scale adds ≥300 GW, non-Li LDES <20% of annual GWh. Falsifier: at least two non-Li 10-hour-plus technologies each reach ≥1 GW cumulative commercial operation (not pilot) before 2030, with third-party availability data.

**2040 deployment trajectory (forecast):** 2026 ~126 GW / 403 GWh adds; 2030 ~184 GW / 665 GWh; 2035 ~236 GW / 1,000 GWh; 2040 ~280 GW / 1,550 GWh (analyst interpolation from BNEF anchors). 2040 cumulative grid-scale ≈ 3.4 TW / 14 TWh. LCOS proxy for 4-hour LFP at the system level tracks Lazard v19's $210–292/MWh unsubsidised range [S77], with global median likely $25–45/MWh below U.S. numbers.

### SCN-B — LDES Diversified: 25%

The high-scale, high-diversification outcome. Triggered when multiple national capacity constructs — UK cap-floor, Italy MACSE, ERCOT/CAISO capacity-credit reform, and Chinese provincial capacity payments — converge on 10–36-hour technologies by 2030–2033. EPRI/LDES Council benchmarking shows intra-day LDES targeting $650/kW at 75% RTE by 2030 versus current $1,100–1,400/kW at 69% RTE, and multi-day targets of $1,100/kW at 55–60% RTE versus $1,900–2,500/kW at 45% RTE today [S14][S19]. BNEF explicitly forecasts LDES competition to emerge but not match Li-ion's cost trajectory [S48]. Mechanism chain: (A) capacity constructs price hours 5–24 separately from hours 1–4 → (B) project finance clears for vanadium/iron flow, A-CAES (Hydrostor, Apex), LAES (Energy Dome [S95]), and iron-air (Form Energy [S98]) at >$1,000/kWh energy module cost because duration-mix shifts revenue to $/kW-yr terms → (C) manufacturing scale-up hits DOE's 3 GW/yr (2030) and 10–15 GW/yr (2035) targets [S88] → (D) 2040 outcome: non-Li ≥20% of annual grid-scale GWh, annual adds ≥300 GW. Falsifier: capacity constructs fail to clear at $50–75/kW-yr for 10-hour technologies before 2032.

**2040 deployment trajectory (forecast):** same gross-volume trajectory as SCN-A, but duration mix shifts to ~45% at >4 hours and ~13% at >8 hours by 2040. LCOS spread between LFP and best-in-class LDES at 12 hours narrows from ~3× today to <1.6×, but LFP at 4 hours remains cheapest per MWh [S72].

### SCN-C — China-Centric Vertical: 20%

China pulls away as both the largest market and the most concentrated supply base. Chinese system integrators already captured roughly 76% of the 2025 global BESS market per Wood Mackenzie, with eight of the top ten China-based [S32]. The 2025–2027 action plan targets ≥180 GW by 2027 and ≈RMB 250 billion direct investment, while explicitly shifting from mandated storage to capacity-market and ancillary-market participation [S34]. BYD's 12.5 GWh Saudi framework plus prior 2.6 GWh [S33] illustrates the export vector. Mechanism chain: (A) Chinese suppliers bundle cells, PCS, EMS, EPC, and 10-year service agreements, lowering LCOE for buyers in Saudi, Chile, and Australia → (B) Western tariff and FEOC barriers slow ex-China deployment growth, capping non-Chinese annual adds below 300 GW globally → (C) capacity construct adoption in the West stalls because IRA/OBBA storage ITC remains legally unsettled per the CESA summary [S55] and 2026 NFPA 855 revisions raise compliance costs [S66][S74] → (D) 2040 outcome: annual global adds <300 GW, non-Li LDES <20% GWh, but China alone accounts for >55% of cumulative GW. Falsifier: a non-Chinese integrator captures >15% of 2028 BESS shipments, or U.S. Treasury's OBBBA Notice on storage ITC clears project finance for >5 GW of non-Li LDES.

**2040 deployment trajectory (forecast):** 2026 ~110 GW / 350 GWh adds; 2030 ~150 GW / 510 GWh; 2040 ~230 GW / 1,150 GWh. China cumulative reaches ~1.7 TW by 2040, non-China ~1.0 TW. LCOS 4-hour China-sourced FOB lands ~30–40% below U.S. delivered cost.

### SCN-D — Stagnation: 25%

Deployment decelerates because the binding constraints are non-technological. U.S. interconnection queues hold ~1,030 GW of storage requests but historically only ~14% of queued capacity completes construction, with average hookup times stretching to ~5 years [S1]. ERCOT revenue compression [S22] chills merchant finance; BNEF's high-tariff scenario already reduces 2025–2027 U.S. build by 51–74% [S6]. India's 102 GWh tendered in 2025 produced only ~0.5 GWh commissioned, exposing execution and DISCOM-credit gaps [S48]. Mechanism chain: (A) revenue cannibalisation + interconnection saturation + financing cost rises compress uncontracted IRRs below debt-service thresholds in 2027–2029 → (B) Chinese cell and system overcapacity (2026 nominal cell capacity potentially 1.7 TWh versus ~600 GWh shipped [S33]) triggers price war that delays ex-China contract finalisation as buyers wait for further declines → (C) Western capacity markets fail to clear on a tech-neutral basis for >4-hour service, so non-Li share stays <20% even though total volume is suppressed → (D) 2040 outcome: annual grid-scale adds <300 GW, non-Li LDES <20% GWh. Falsifier: cumulative global grid-scale additions exceed 2.5 TW by 2032, signalling the 300 GW/yr threshold is breached early.

**2040 deployment trajectory (forecast):** 2026 ~105 GW / 320 GWh adds; 2030 ~140 GW / 470 GWh; 2040 ~210 GW / 950 GWh. Cumulative ≈ 2.4 TW / 9 TWh. 4-hour LCOS in $/MWh terms stays flat-to-rising in real terms outside China because higher financing cost offsets cell-cost decline [S77].

### Resolution criteria (applies to all four)

Scenarios are mutually exclusive on the 2×2 axis (annual grid-scale GW ≥300 GW by 2040; non-Li LDES ≥20% of 2040 annual GWh) and collectively exhaustive. Probabilities sum to 100%. Material evidence to watch: PJM 2027/28 BRA clearing price; Treasury OBBA storage-ITC implementing notice; AEMO 2026 ISP final release; EPRI/LDES Council 2027 update; and 2027 China NEA installed-base print versus the 180 GW target [S34][S68].

### Contradiction log

Five material inter-source conflicts are unresolved as of the cutoff and must be carried into the synthesis as explicit disagreements rather than averaged silently.

1. **Global 2025 adds: BNEF vs IEA.** BNEF reports 112 GW/307 GWh of battery storage additions in 2025 (utility-scale plus behind-the-meter combined in the headline) [S2]. IEA's older net-zero tracker implies a slower curve. The gap is methodological: BNEF counts all battery storage including C&I and residential, whereas IEA's stated 85 GW operating battery fleet at end-2023 is narrower in scope [S10][S11]. The synthesis section uses BNEF 112 GW/307 GWh as the 2025 anchor and flags the IEA boundary explicitly.

2. **China end-2025 new-type storage: NEA vs CNESA.** NEA reports 136 GW/351 GWh cumulative, 96.1% lithium, average 2.58h [S68]. CNESA, republished via the same ministry channel, cites 144.7 GW and a 51.9% global share, implying a global cumulative of roughly 279 GW [S9]. The 8.7 GW delta reflects statistical cut-off, project definition ("commissioned" vs "grid-connected"), and CNESA's inclusion of demonstration projects. The synthesis uses NEA 136 GW as the official anchor and treats CNESA 144.7 GW as a high-end alternative reading, never averaging the two.

3. **ERCOT vs PJM revenue model.** Modo reports ERCOT battery revenue collapsed from roughly US$192/kW-year in 2023 to US$55/kW-year in 2024 — a 71% one-year decline driven by ancillary-service saturation [S22][S23]. PJM's 2027/2028 Base Residual Auction procured 134,311 MW at a clearing price reset, indicating scarcity pricing re-emerged after capacity-market reform, not revenue collapse [S47]. The two markets point in opposite directions on storage asset profitability in the same year; the synthesis must hold ERCOT and PJM as separate case studies rather than generalize a US revenue trend.

4. **DOE Liftoff vs EPRI unit mismatch.** DOE Liftoff states intra-day LDES targets of US$650/kW with 75% RTE by 2030, and multi-day targets of US$1,100/kW with 55–60% RTE [S88]. EPRI's cost references use $/kWh with a different assumed duration, producing figures that look comparable but are not. Any LCOS chart must normalize both to either $/kW-year or $/MWh-cycle under an identical duration and round-trip efficiency assumption; the synthesis section presents the DOE figures only in $/kW plus RTE pairs, never re-cast into EPRI's $/kWh frame.

5. **Sodium-ion announced vs shipped capacity.** Vendor announcements (HiNa, Alsym 9 GWh framework, multiple 4 GWh US plant plans) describe nameplate capacity and forward agreements [S44]. Benchmark/Energy-Storage.news reports 2025 BESS cell shipments near 600 GWh and system shipments near 460 GWh, dominated by LFP, with sodium-ion shipment volumes not separately broken out at the gigawatt-hour level [S33]. The announced-to-shipped gap for sodium-ion is therefore a S3 confidence claim, and the synthesis treats every sodium-ion capacity figure as a nameplate or framework number, never as a 2025–2026 deployment figure.
