## The Two-Regime World: What the Simulation Reveals About 2026–2031

The simulation did not render either of the two futures the popular debate keeps reaching for. There was no clean decoupling into rival technospheres, and no restoration of frictionless globalization. What the god's-eye run actually produced was stranger and more instructive: two structural regimes snapping into place at the same moment and then locking each other in. The first is a durable elevated-tariff regime—the US effective rate settling just above 11% after the SCOTUS strike-down of IEEPA tariffs as of 2026-04-01 [S2-a], down sharply from the ~28% Liberation Day peak [S1-i] but never falling back toward the pre-2018 low-single digits. The second is an AI compute-scarcity regime, with Big-4 hyperscaler capex guiding to $700–725B in 2026, up roughly 77% from $410B in 2025 [S3-d]. Neither regime is a passing shock. Both are now load-bearing structures the agents build their strategies on top of, and the entire decade plays out inside the space where they overlap.

The single most important thing the simulation reveals is that these two regimes are not running on separate tracks. AI compute became the hinge connecting them—and it did so by taking on two contradictory roles at once. The knowledge graph captures this duality with unusual clarity. On one side, compute is a mercantilist *weapon*: the Trump administration "controls the AI-chip valve through Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) licensing," and BIS in turn "denies DeepSeek access to advanced tools and chips via export controls" (依据：Bureau of Industry and Security --[SANCTIONS]--> DeepSeek). On the other side, the very same compute is a mercantilist *prize*—something states and firms spend hundreds of billions to court and hoard. Bridgewater's framing, carried into the simulation as an anchoring characterization, is that the buildout is

> "the largest capital-expenditure expansion since World War II, producing a compute 'rationing system' in which actors who fail to secure compute are competitively eliminated."

(依据：Bridgewater Associates --[CHARACTERIZES]--> AI compute). Hold those two roles together and the emergent logic of the decade falls out: if compute is simultaneously the thing you deny your rival and the thing you cannot afford to be denied, then every actor is forced into the same posture—weaponize where you hold the chokepoint, and frantically secure supply everywhere you are exposed. That is not a recipe for decoupling. It is a recipe for dense, hostile interdependence.

**Why the two regimes reinforce rather than cancel each other.** The feedback loop the simulation exposes runs in both directions. Tariffs and export controls raise the strategic value of domestic compute, which drives the capex boom higher; the capex boom, in turn, raises the value of the chokepoints—rare earths, HBM, EUV lithography, advanced fabrication—that the tariff regime is fighting over. China "controls the rare-earth and magnet chokepoint, giving it a coercive supply-chain veto" (依据：People's Republic of China --[CONTROLS]--> rare earths), while the United States "constrains China via its chokepoint over advanced fabrication tools and chips." Each side's weapon is the other side's vulnerability. The graph shows the mutual sanction structure explicitly and symmetrically (依据：United States --[SANCTIONS]--> People's Republic of China; People's Republic of China --[SANCTIONS]--> United States). This is the mechanism behind "contested equilibrium": neither side can push decoupling to completion because each holds the other hostage, and the shared dependency on a single supply chain—TSMC's leading-edge fabrication, ASML's EUV tools, China's magnet processing—means the cost of a clean break is mutual ruin.

**The texture of the future: surveillance over declaration.** The activity signals from the run give this equilibrium a distinct behavioral signature. Across the simulation, FOLLOW actions outnumbered CREATE_POST by 618 to 40—a better than 15-to-1 ratio. This is the numeric fingerprint of a world that watches far more than it declares. Agents are not issuing manifestos or announcing decisive breaks; they are monitoring each other, hedging, and waiting to see who moves first. The People's Republic of China led every agent in the simulation with 42 total actions—38 of them FOLLOW and only 4 CREATE_POST—the profile of a challenger relentlessly surveilling the equilibrium's edges rather than openly rupturing it. That behavioral pattern is exactly what "managed interdependence" looks like from the inside: not détente, not war, but perpetual mutual observation, each actor probing for the moment a chokepoint can be squeezed or must be reinforced.

The tempo reinforces the reading. Activity exploded from 20 actions in the opening round to 638 by round 10—the peak round drew in 80 active agents at once. The collision is not a slow drift; it is a rapid, near-simultaneous scramble in which nearly every actor in the system activates together. When one node moves on compute or tariffs, the whole network responds in the same round. That is the density of interdependence made visible: there is no quiet corner of the board.

**No one could leave the room.** The most structurally revealing result is the faction map. Clustering agents by shared interaction targets—the most objective test of who is entangled with whom—the simulation produced exactly one coalition of 80 members. Not two blocs. Not a US-led sphere facing a China-led sphere. One cluster, containing the People's Republic of China, the US Federal Executive, the European Union, Nvidia, TSMC, DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC, the US hyperscalers, Taiwan's government, and the Roberts Court all together. Rivals and allies share the same interaction network because they are fighting over the same handful of assets. China's DeepSeek is starved by American BIS controls yet leans on domestic Huawei Ascend silicon (依据：DeepSeek --[USES]--> Ascend); the American hyperscalers depend on Nvidia (依据：US Hyperscalers --[CUSTOMER_OF]--> Nvidia); Nvidia and everyone else depend on TSMC, which depends on Dutch ASML for EUV (依据：ASML --[SUPPLIES]--> TSMC). When the dependency chains all converge on the same nodes, clean blocs become topologically impossible. The single-cluster result is the simulation's blunt verdict on the decoupling thesis: you cannot decouple from a network you cannot leave.

**A necessary counterpoint.** One could object that a single 80-agent cluster is an artifact of how densely the scenario was seeded—that if the model had been given more autonomy, blocs would have crystallized. It is a fair challenge, and the honest answer is that the simulation cannot fully rule it out. But the objection actually cuts the other way on the substance. The reason the agents share one interaction network is not arbitrary wiring; it is that the real chokepoints genuinely do converge. There is one sole-scale leading-edge foundry. There is one dominant EUV supplier. There is one country processing ~70%+ of rare-earth magnets. So long as those physical bottlenecks persist—and the reshoring evidence says leading-edge parity is a ~2030 story at best—the pressure toward a single contested network is structural, not an artifact. Blocs would require duplicating the entire stack, which no actor can do within the horizon.

This is why the decade is best understood as a contested equilibrium rather than a resolution. The tariff regime and the compute regime did not fight to a winner in the simulation; they fused into a single terrain on which every subsequent scenario is played. The elevated-11% tariff floor and the $700B+ capex ceiling define the box. Inside that box, the four futures priced in the next chapter—managed muddle-through, mercantilist escalation, capex bust, and efficiency-driven multipolarity—are not alternatives to the two-regime world. They are the four ways the pressure inside that box can break. The overwhelming FOLLOW-over-CREATE behavior, the single dense coalition, and the round-10 activity spike together argue that the modal outcome is not rupture but a taut, watchful, permanently negotiated interdependence—one in which the most consequential question is no longer "will they decouple?" but "who controls the chokepoint when the truce expires?"

## The Forecasting Framework: Cause-and-Effect Engine and How the Four Futures Were Priced

The four forecasts in this report are not four independent bets. They are four regions of a single outcome space, carved out by the same set of coupled tensions, and priced against the same evidence base. Before defending any scenario individually, this chapter lays out the cause-and-effect engine underneath all of them—and shows why the simulation's own behavior forced the probabilities to land where they did rather than at the tidy 25%-each a lazy partition would produce.

**The engine: four tensions, tightly coupled**

The collision runs on four axes, and the central analytical claim of this framework is that they are *not* separable—a movement on one propagates into the others. The simulation made that coupling visible in its causal graph rather than leaving it as assertion.

The first axis is *efficiency versus security*. Cost-minimizing sourcing pulls toward global supply chains; national-security logic pulls toward reshoring and control. The simulation encodes this as a live contradiction inside single actors: the United States simultaneously subsidizes reshoring and denies inputs to rivals (per: United States --[INVESTS_IN]--> TSMC-Arizona; United States --[SANCTIONS]--> People's Republic of China). One simulation-derived fact captures the ambiguity that keeps this axis unresolved:

> "The United States subsidizes TSMC-Arizona as part of an effort to reshore advanced fabrication, though it is debated whether this helps the US or merely de-risks Taiwan."

The second axis is *compute abundance versus rationing*. The Big-4 hyperscalers are guiding to $700–725B in 2026 capex, up ~77% from $410B in 2025 [S3-d]—an abundance push—yet export licensing turns that same compute into a rationed asset. The simulation adopts Bridgewater's framing as a structural fact:

> "Bridgewater Associates' CIOs frame the AI compute buildout as the largest capital-expenditure expansion since World War II, producing a compute 'rationing system' in which actors who fail to secure compute are competitively eliminated."

That "rationing system" language is load-bearing for the pricing, and I flag it honestly as resting on secondary summaries [S3-b][S3-c] rather than a verbatim primary text—so it informs the structure but is not treated as settled quantitative fact.

The third axis is *decoupling versus mutual chokepoint hostage-taking*. This is the axis the simulation graph renders most densely. The United States holds the advanced-tool and chip valve (per: Bureau of Industry and Security --[SANCTIONS]--> DeepSeek; United States --[CONSTRAINS]--> People's Republic of China), while China holds the rare-earth and magnet chokepoint (per: People's Republic of China --[CONTROLS]--> rare earths; People's Republic of China --[CONSTRAINS]--> United States). Critically, the graph also records the *counter-dependencies* that stop full decoupling—China needs US soybeans and market access (per: People's Republic of China --[DEPENDS_ON]--> soybeans), and both sides need TSMC intact (per: TSMC --[CONSTRAINS]--> People's Republic of China). Neither can pull its lever without bleeding.

The fourth axis is the *capex boom racing a self-imposed monetization deadline*. The buildout is financed in the same bond market straining under ~$1T of FY2026 federal net interest [S3-a], while 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots delivered zero measurable ROI as of August 2025 [S2-f]. The debt is, as one source frames it, "an AI capex story" [S3-a]. This gives the boom an 18–36 month clock: monetization must arrive before financing conditions tighten.

**Why the axes had to be modeled together**

The reason these are coupled, not additive, is that a shock on one axis reprices the others. A security-driven tightening on axis three (say, a truce lapse) instantly hardens rationing on axis two, which raises input costs on axis one and pulls the monetization deadline on axis four forward. The simulation exposes this transmission structurally: export controls do not merely restrict—they *accelerate* the rival stack (per: export controls --[ACCELERATES]--> SMIC), meaning a move meant to widen the US lead can feed the very multipolar outcome that erodes it. That single edge is why Scenario Two (escalation) and Scenario Four (efficiency-diffusion) are not opposites; escalation is one of the mechanisms that *produces* diffusion.

**What the simulation's behavior tells us about the base rate**

The pricing did not come from intuition; it was disciplined by how the agents actually behaved. Two aggregate signals dominate.

First, the action mix. Across the run, agents took 618 FOLLOW actions against only 40 CREATE_POST actions—a roughly 15:1 ratio of watching to declaring. In a simulation of a geopolitical-economic system, that is the fingerprint of hedging and mutual surveillance, not open rupture. Actors are monitoring each other's moves and positioning, not committing to irreversible declarations. A world dominated by watching is a world that muddles through; a world dominated by declaration is one that escalates. The 15:1 ratio is the single strongest quantitative argument for making managed interdependence the modal path.

Second, the faction structure. The coalition map returned a single 80-agent cluster containing all the principals at once—People's Republic of China, US Federal Executive, Nvidia, TSMC, US Hyperscalers, OpenAI/Stargate, DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC, the European Union, Taiwan, and the courts, all in the same interaction network. There was no clean second bloc. When even declared rivals share one interaction cluster, comprehensive decoupling is structurally hard to reach—the interdependence is too dense. This is the empirical spine under the 8–15% tariff band assumption and the "no comprehensive decoupling" clause of the modal scenario.

**How the four futures partition the space**

The scenarios are defined so that they collectively cover the outcome space and are distinguished by explicit, falsifiable resolution criteria rather than by mood.

- *Managed Interdependence Muddle-Through (39%)* is TRUE by 2031-06-30 if the US effective tariff rate stays within roughly the 8–15% band (no sustained >20%, no return to <5%) and no comprehensive decoupling occurs. It is anchored on the post-SCOTUS effective rate settling just above 11% as of 2026-04-01 [S2-a]—already inside the band—and on the managed 2025 truce.

- *Mercantilist Escalation / Hard Bifurcation (23%)* is TRUE if any two of: the truce lapses without renewal or new controls are imposed after November 2026; the effective tariff rate is sustained above 20% for 12+ months; comprehensive bifurcation of the chip/AI stack occurs.

- *AI Capex Correction / Financing Bust (19%)* is TRUE if any of: aggregate Big-tech AI capex declines >30% year-over-year in at least one year; a broad AI-infrastructure equity drawdown exceeding ~40% sustains.

- *Efficiency-Diffusion Multipolar (19%)* is TRUE if at least three of: leading open-weight models sit within ~6 months of the frontier closed model on 2+ public benchmarks; Chinese open models set global defaults; indigenous silicon matures; export controls leak materially.

**How the probabilities were assigned**

The 39% modal weight follows directly from the two aggregate signals above—the 15:1 watch-to-declare ratio and the single 80-agent cluster—reinforced by the fact that the load-bearing tariff number already sits inside the resolution band as of the as-of date. Managed interdependence is not the optimistic scenario; it is the *inertial* one, the path the system takes if no chokepoint is actually pulled.

The 23% escalation weight is set by a scheduled flashpoint: the rare-earth truce expiry on November 27, 2026. The simulation's most active agent was People's Republic of China at 42 total actions (38 FOLLOW, 4 CREATE_POST)—the tell of a challenger relentlessly probing the equilibrium's edges rather than accepting it. That probing behavior justifies a materially elevated escalation weight. But it is capped below the base case because the graph shows both chokepoints remain load-bearing and mutual (per: People's Republic of China --[CONSTRAINS]--> United States, alongside United States --[CONSTRAINS]--> People's Republic of China): each side is hostage to the other, which is a de-escalation incentive, not an escalation one.

The two 19% tail weights are deliberately equal because they represent opposite failure modes of the *same* capex bet. The financing bust is the demand-side failure (the $700B doesn't monetize before the bond market tightens); the efficiency-diffusion path is the supply-side obsolescence (DeepSeek-style efficiency makes the hoarded compute and the export moat worth less). The simulation supports both being live: DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC tied for third-most-active at 26 actions—a challenger fully engaged, not marginalized—and one fact states plainly that "DeepSeek's open-weight diffusion undercuts the value of export bans and export controls by eroding the advantage of hoarding closed frontier models." Meanwhile the ROI gap (95% zero-return pilots [S2-f]) keeps the bust live. Neither dominates the other on current evidence, so they are priced level.

**The honest disagreement inside the pricing**

The sharpest objection to this partition is that 19% for the capex bust is too low—that a >$700B/yr boom financed on record 2025 AI-linked bond issuance of $220B [S3-a], against 95% pilot failure, is a textbook bubble that deserves 30%+. Steelmanned: if diffusion follows the historical dynamo-and-computer J-curve [S1-g], productivity shows up decades late, and the debt clock (~$1T net interest [S3-a]) does not grant decades. That is a serious case.

The reason the pricing holds at 19% is the resolution bar itself. The criterion is not "ROI disappoints"—it is a >30% YoY capex *decline* or a sustained >40% equity drawdown. The simulation shows the buildout is defended by deep, interlocking vendor-financing commitments (per: Microsoft/Oracle/SoftBank --[FUNDS]--> OpenAI/Stargate; OpenAI/Stargate --[CUSTOMER_OF]--> Nvidia) and by hyperscalers actively vertically integrating to protect margins (per: Amazon --[HAS_CUSTOM_SILICON]--> Trainium; Google --[HAS_CUSTOM_SILICON]--> TPU). Committed capital of this structure tends to slow before it collapses; a disappointing-but-continuing boom resolves the criterion FALSE. The bubble thesis can be right about ROI and still not clear the bar—which is precisely why the weight sits at 19% and not higher.

**The evidence hierarchy behind every number**

The pricing is disciplined by a source ladder. S1 (primary/authoritative—Yale Budget Lab, CRS, TSMC filings, IEA) carries the load-bearing quantitative facts: the effective tariff rate just above 11% post-SCOTUS (2026-04-01) [S2-a] against the 28% Liberation Day peak (2025-04-13) [S1-i]; TSMC's $165B Arizona commitment (2025-03-01) [S1-k]; data-center electricity demand rising from 415 TWh (2024) toward a projected 945 TWh (2030) [S1]. S2 (reliable secondary—Tax Foundation, Brookings, MIT NANDA) supplies the contested interpretive claims: the $160B in IEEPA tariffs ruled illegally collected (2026-02-20) [S2-a], the 95% pilot-failure figure [S2-f]. S3 (media—CNBC, Reuters, SCMP) supplies the fast-moving quantities like the $700–725B 2026 capex guidance [S3-d] and the truce-durability dispute. Where a claim rests only on S3 secondary summaries—the Bridgewater "rationing" thesis most notably—it shapes the framework's structure but is explicitly not treated as verified fact. The governing rule is simple: every load-bearing number is either traceable to an [S] source at its as-of date, or flagged as a simulation-derived signal—and the two are never blended.

That separation is the discipline that lets these four probabilities be defended rather than merely asserted—and it is the standard the four per-scenario chapters that follow are held to.

## Scenario One — Managed Interdependence Muddle-Through (39%, Modal Path)

The single most important thing the simulation reveals about the modal path is a behavioral tell that is easy to miss: across the entire run, agents overwhelmingly chose to watch rather than to speak. The action ledger records 618 FOLLOW actions against just 40 CREATE_POST actions — a roughly 15-to-1 ratio of surveillance to declaration. In a world genuinely bifurcating into two hostile camps, you would expect the opposite: loud, repeated position-taking as each bloc rallies its side. Instead the future the model played out is one of mutual, wary monitoring — everyone keeping tabs on everyone, nobody willing to burn the bridge. That texture, more than any single quote, is why muddle-through is priced as the center of gravity at 39%, and it is the argument this chapter defends.

**The trajectory of the most active agent is the equilibrium in miniature**

The clearest microcosm is the People's Republic of China, the single most active agent in the entire simulation at 42 actions. Its round-by-round trajectory is revealing: in round 0 it acted only by broadcasting — CREATE_POST×2, pure declaration. By round 10 its behavior had inverted into FOLLOW×38 with CREATE_POST×2. In other words, the challenger stopped announcing and started watching — 38 acts of monitoring the network's other nodes versus two of speaking. This is exactly the posture of a player who has decided the game is worth staying inside rather than flipping the table: probe the edges, track every counterparty, keep the leverage sheathed. It maps precisely onto what the research archive calls Beijing's "calibrated escalate-then-negotiate pattern holding leverage in reserve." The simulation did not just assert managed interdependence; it produced the behavioral signature of it in the actions of the agent most motivated to break it.

The interviewed China agent stated the doctrine explicitly. Asked whether the equilibrium survives to 2031, the simulation agent put its own probability below the incumbents':

> Simulation agent "People's Republic of China" projected: "We hold this at four-to-five in ten — it is the product of everyone choosing the lesser of two evils, not a stable institutional arrangement… the rare-earth and magnet card, the one-year suspension, we treat as leverage held in reserve, not surrendered — a pillar we can pull out at any time."

That is the crucial distinction for pricing this scenario. Suspension is not resolution. The truce holds not because the weapons were destroyed but because both sides decided, round after round, that firing costs more than the toll.

**Why the reciprocal hostage structure makes this the base case**

The structural reason muddle-through dominates is symmetric, un-exitable vulnerability. The knowledge graph encodes it as two opposing constraint edges that cancel into a standoff: the US constrains China through advanced fabrication tools and chips (依据：United States --[CONSTRAINS]--> People's Republic of China), while China constrains the US through rare-earth and magnet dependence (依据：People's Republic of China --[CONSTRAINS]--> United States). The archive is blunt about the physical asymmetry:

> "China holds a chokepoint over the United States through rare-earth and magnet dependence, potentially retaining a coercive veto over Western supply chains."

Neither chokepoint has a fast exit. Leading-edge reshoring is a ~2030 story; a non-China rare-earth and magnet supply chain remains, in the archive's own framing, an unresolved open question. When both weapons are loaded and neither can be disarmed in the horizon, the dominant strategy is to threaten but not fire. The US-side interview agent converged on the same number from the opposite chair:

> Simulation agent "US Federal Executive (Trump Administration)" projected: "I'd put the odds of this managed-interdependence equilibrium surviving to 2031 at about five-to-six in ten — more fragile than markets think, but more resilient than the doomsayers assume. The reason is simple: both sides need it. China needs our high-end compute and our end market; we need their rare earths and magnets, and we need Nvidia's China revenue."

Note the convergence: the challenger says four-to-five, the incumbent says five-to-six, and every other interviewed party clustered in the same band. That spread — roughly 40–60% survival across adversaries with every incentive to talk their own book — is itself evidence for a probability mass concentrated but short of majority. It is why 39%, not 60%.

**The revenue-share valve: mercantilism digesting AI rather than colliding with it**

The most inventive adaptation the simulation surfaces is the redesign of the export valve from a binary switch into a metered toll. Rather than choosing between banning Nvidia from China (forfeiting the market) or freeing it (forfeiting control), the administration built an instrument that does both. The graph records the mechanism directly (依据：Nvidia --[INFLUENCES]--> US Federal Executive (Trump Administration)), and the archive quantifies it: the administration "extracts a 25% chip revenue share from Nvidia via the revenue-share model and BIS licensing over chip access." The Nvidia agent described the H20 arc — banned, then approved, then revenue-shared — as proof of the whole equilibrium's logic:

> Simulation agent "Nvidia" projected: "We lived through the full swing in 2025 — H20 from banned, to approved, to revenue-share — and that is precisely what proves 'deals' are more durable than 'rules.'… once China feels it no longer needs our chips, the revenue-share lubricant stops working."

That last clause names the scenario's expiry condition from the commercial side rather than the political one. The valve works only while China still wants the silicon. This is mercantilism absorbing AI: the state monetizes the chokepoint instead of merely wielding it, and every party — Washington collecting rents, Nvidia keeping the market, Beijing buying time — has a reason to keep the machine running. Locally rational deals that make defection individually expensive are the microfoundation of a 39% base case.

**Both sides entrench at once — the contested-equilibrium signature**

What makes this "contested" rather than settled is that the truce is precisely the window in which each side reduces the dependence that forced the truce. The archive shows China building the parallel stack — "a parallel chip-fabrication stack around SMIC" (依据：People's Republic of China --[BACKS]--> SMIC) and "a parallel AI stack around DeepSeek… to have Chinese open models set global defaults" (依据：People's Republic of China --[BACKS]--> DeepSeek) — while the same controls meant to contain it are argued to feed it:

> "US export controls are argued to accelerate the indigenous Chinese chip stack built around SMIC."

Simultaneously the Western incumbents deepen their own moat. TSMC's agent described the Arizona buildout not as commerce but as geopolitical hedging, and — strikingly — nominated itself as a winner:

> Simulation agent "TSMC" projected: "In a muddle-through, the biggest winners are whoever holds the irreplaceable node — frankly, that includes us: nearly 90% of leading-edge capacity is still in Taiwan, nobody can route around it, and our bargaining power is actually amplified by the delay… a half-finished equilibrium rewards monopoly and punishes sameness."

## Scenario Two — Mercantilist Escalation and Hard Bifurcation (23%, The Truce-Expiry Tail)

The simulation prices a one-in-four chance that the managed truce does not hold—and the most revealing evidence for that weight is not what the agents said, but what China *did*. Across the run, the People's Republic of China was the single most active agent of all, logging 42 actions. Yet only 4 of those were CREATE_POST; the other 38 were FOLLOW. The behavioral trajectory is even sharper: in the opening round China broadcast (CREATE_POST×2), then in the peak round 10 it went almost entirely to surveillance—FOLLOW×38 against just 2 new posts. In a simulation where the global action mix was 618 FOLLOW to 40 CREATE_POST, the challenger that watches most and declares least is not disengaging. It is doing reconnaissance on the equilibrium's edges—monitoring every counterparty, pricing every move, holding its own cards face-down. That is the posture of a state preparing to probe, not to settle. This scenario reads that tell forward.

**The scheduled flashpoint: why a calendar date, not a slow drift, is the mechanism.** Modern Mercantilism has done something unusual—it has written the next crisis onto the calendar. China suspended its rare-earth and magnet export controls for exactly one year, trading the pause for tech-control relief and tariff stability "while holding the leverage in reserve" [S3-f]. That reserve expires on November 27, 2026. The scenario resolves TRUE by mid-2031 if any two of: the truce lapses with no renewal or new controls are imposed after November 2026; the US effective tariff rate is sustained above 20% for twelve months or more; or comprehensive bilateral controls re-tighten. The critical insight from the simulation is that the agents themselves converge on the date as the ignition point rather than any gradual erosion.

Simulation agent "peoples_republic_of_china" projected the dynamic in almost mechanical terms:

> "If it breaks before 2031, the rupture will not be a slow drift—it will be a phase transition ignited by some identifiable event. At that point the rare-earth and magnet leverage we kept in reserve will very likely be reactivated as reciprocal retaliation, and the rhythm of 'escalate-then-negotiate' will be broken into 'escalate-then-escalate.'"

Simulation agent "nvidia" independently flagged the same node:

> "I'd watch the November 27, 2026 truce-expiry point in particular—if rare-earth controls and export controls tighten at the same time, with no new deal to succeed them, that could form a cascade."

This is the escalation engine: a mutual chokepoint war that has been paused, not disarmed. China's side of the chokepoint is the rare-earth and magnet processing monopoly—roughly 70%+ of global capacity—backed by the proven willingness to weaponize it. The research record shows the sequence is not hypothetical: China imposed export licensing on gallium and germanium starting August 2023 [S3-i], then escalated to a full embargo on US shipments by December 2024, exploiting a downstream monopoly (依据：People's Republic of China --[REGULATES]--> gallium; --[REGULATES]--> germanium). The US side is the advanced-tool and HBM chokepoint: BIS licensing over EUV/DUV lithography and high-bandwidth memory, the inputs "required for every accelerator" [research]. In escalation, each side reaches for the lever it controls—China constrains the United States through rare-earth dependence, the United States constrains China through advanced fabrication tools and chips on which China remains dependent (依据：People's Republic of China --[CONSTRAINS]--> United States; United States --[CONSTRAINS]--> People's Republic of China).

**Why escalation is a real risk and not just rhetoric.** The structural incentive to defect is genuine because both parties are racing to erode the *other's* leverage before their own runs out. Simulation agent "beijing" was explicit that the truce is a countdown, not a settlement:

> "This is not a steady state—it's a ceasefire with an expiry date. It's structurally fragile because both sides are racing against the clock to weaken the other's bargaining chips: we're advancing our indigenous stack through DeepSeek, Huawei, and SMIC, while the other side fills in alternative sources of critical minerals. The moment either side believes 'I no longer need you,' the equilibrium instantly loses its support."

This maps to the controversy the research base surfaces honestly. On one side, President Trump's framing that the deal "will go on long beyond the year" [S3-n]—optimism that managed interdependence has become self-sustaining. On the other, SCMP's reading of a "fragile truce" built on "divergent accounts and structural rivalry" [S3-g], where both chokepoints remain load-bearing and each renewal is a fresh negotiation rather than a settled fact. The simulation's China agent sits closer to SCMP than to Trump: it treats the one-year pause as "reserved leverage, not a waiver."

**The tariff leg of the escalation.** The scenario does not require a chokepoint war alone; a sustained tariff shock above 20% for twelve-plus months independently satisfies part of the resolution logic. That threshold is not far-fetched—the effective rate peaked near 28% during the April 2025 Liberation Day reciprocal tariffs [S1-i] before the SCOTUS strike-down pushed it back to just above 11% [S2-a]. An escalation path reactivates Section 232/301 authorities and pushes back toward the 20%+ band. Crucially, the tariff and chokepoint legs are correlated: a truce collapse over rare earths gives the US political cover to re-escalate tariffs, and a tariff re-escalation gives China the pretext to reactivate its mineral leverage. The two forces amplify rather than offset. Simulation agent "global_south/connectors" identified precisely why calendar-anchored escalation is so dangerous:

> "What I fear most are exactly these 'calendar-type' nodes, because markets and politicians will both game around them, and the margin for error is very small. If the renewal fails, tightened rare-earth controls stacked on a new round of tariffs would be the most realistic trigger."

**Winners: whoever holds the more inelastic chokepoint.** In a hard bifurcation, the payoff structure inverts from the muddle-through case. The winner is not the most integrated player but the one whose chokepoint the other side cannot route around within the horizon. Here the asymmetry cuts partly in China's favor on the mineral axis—simulation agent "european_commission" conceded that a "rare-earth or magnet export-control escalation would expose our industrial soft spot within weeks," a vulnerability the EU cannot fix at the speed of a single decision because "our fiscal system is fragmented and decisions need 27-country consensus." China constrains the EU directly through the bloc's rare-earth and magnet dependence (依据：People's Republic of China --[CONSTRAINS]--> European Union; European Union --[DEPENDS_ON]--> People's Republic of China). But the US chokepoint is also inelastic on the advanced-node axis: EUV denial to SMIC caps China's ability to close the frontier gap (依据：ASML --[CONSTRAINS]--> DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC). The result of escalation is mutual damage—neither chokepoint is elastic, so both sides bleed, and the "winner" is merely whoever bleeds slower.

**Losers: TSMC and globally-integrated firms, with the Taiwan contingency as the sharpest downside.** The cascade analysis exposes the tail's worst node. The path from China to TSMC runs through the security triangle: People's Republic of China --[OPPOSES]--> Taiwan (Government) --[STEWARDS]--> the silicon shield and TSMC. TSMC is the central node whose disruption in a Taiwan conflict would sever the entire advanced-chip supply for both blocs simultaneously. Simulation agent "tsmc" named this as the fear no hedge can cover:

> "The most likely trigger is a substantive escalation across the Taiwan Strait—that is the tail risk none of our hedging can fully cover, and it is what I worry about most... What keeps me up at night are the geopolitical variables we don't get to decide."

TSMC's response—raising its Arizona commitment from $12B to $65B to $165B [research, S1-k]—is described by the agent itself as "geographic hedging, not a pure commercial decision," an insurance policy against exactly this scenario. But the hedge is incomplete: leading-edge 2nm/1.6nm production stays in Taiwan through ~2030, so a near-term contingency would still crater global compute. Globally-integrated mid-stream manufacturers—those forced to build duplicate supply chains across two blocs without a technology moat—are the other clear losers, punished by an equilibrium that "rewards monopoly and punishes sameness," as the TSMC agent put it.

**Why 23%, and not higher.** The scenario is a live tail, not the modal path, because both chokepoints are load-bearing in both directions—and that mutual hostage-taking is itself a powerful de-escalation incentive. The simulation's China agent is candid about the constraints that cap its own aggression:

> "My constraints are clear: the local-debt cleanup is still incomplete, there are hidden liabilities, demographic and growth pressures, and I still need the other side's soybeans and market—so I'm unwilling to trigger full decoupling before self-reliance matures. The weight of my worry equals exactly the distance I still am from self-sufficiency."

That is the pricing logic in one sentence. China remains dependent on TSMC-tier fabrication and on US soybeans and market access (依据：People's Republic of China --[DEPENDS_ON]--> TSMC; --[DEPENDS_ON]--> soybeans), which limits how far it can push. The US, symmetrically, cannot cut off China's rare-earth supply because it does not control it. Each side's chokepoint is also its restraint: reaching for the lever invites a symmetric counter-strike on an input it cannot replace within the horizon. The calibrated "escalate-then-negotiate" pattern that ran from 2023 gallium licensing through the 2024 embargo to the 2025 truce is the base-rate behavior; escalation requires that pattern to break into "escalate-then-escalate," which needs a trigger sharp enough to override the mutual-dependence brake—most plausibly a Taiwan military event or a simultaneous, uncoordinated tightening at the November 2026 expiry. Those are real but not likely enough to be modal. 23% captures a scenario that is scheduled, structurally supported by a relentlessly probing challenger, and genuinely destabilizing—while still restrained by the fact that in a chokepoint war where both sides hold a knife to the other's throat, the rational first move remains, narrowly, to renew.

## Scenario Three — AI Capex Correction and Financing Bust (19%, The Bond-Market Reckoning)

The financing-bust scenario is the least intuitive of the four futures and, in the simulation, the most quietly conceded. Every institution positioned along the money chain—from the central bank to the chip designer to the most leveraged borrower—named the risk unprompted and diagnosed its mechanism with a precision no genuine bull would volunteer. That combination of high salience among the exposed and specific, self-reported failure logic is why this branch earns a serious 19% rather than a dismissible tail. It is not a story about AI failing to work. It is a story about the calendar: a ~$700–725B/yr buildout, up ~77% from ~$410B in 2025 [S3-d], financed against a bond market already straining under roughly $1 trillion in FY2026 federal net interest [S3-a], colliding with a monetization curve that MIT NANDA's finding—95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots delivering zero measurable ROI [S2-f]—suggests may arrive a few quarters too late. The debt, as the research dossier frames it, is "an AI capex story" [S3-a]. In this branch, the story ends with a repricing.

**The disagreement is over how much rests on real cash flow—and it is wide**

The first tell from the simulation is that the parties cannot agree on how much of the buildout is self-funded. Asked what share of the ~$700B is covered by operating cash flow, the simulated Federal Reserve put the core players at "sixty to seventy percent"; Nvidia said the figure is "higher than the market fears"; Bridgewater estimated "about half"; and both OpenAI/Stargate and SoftBank gave a far more pessimistic "thirty to forty percent." The hyperscalers themselves—the demand engine—conceded the ratio is falling fast:

> Simulated agent "US Hyperscalers" projected: "The share currently covered by operating cash flow is declining rapidly. Capex growth—2026 combined guidance of roughly $700–725B, up about 77% from ~$410B in 2025—has clearly outrun cash-flow growth, so free cash flow is compressed and even turning negative; Amazon alone projects around -$17B of FCF. The gap is filled by record bond issuance—about $121B in 2025, four times the five-year average—and by circular investment arrangements between chip and model partners."

A four-to-seven-hundred-billion-dollar range of disagreement over the most basic solvency question, voiced by the very actors funding it, is itself the signature of an unstable structure. When financiers cannot agree what fraction of their own spending is self-supporting, the residual is being carried by faith in future demand.

**Nvidia named the accelerant: the circular-financing loop**

The most striking moment in the interviews was the contested asset at the center of the entire collision admitting its own reflexivity. Nvidia—whose chips gate the buildout (依据：US Hyperscalers --[CUSTOMER_OF]--> Nvidia; OpenAI/Stargate --[CUSTOMER_OF]--> Nvidia)—did not deflect the loop; it mapped it:

> Simulated agent "Nvidia" projected: "The edge of the buildout increasingly relies on debt, leasing structures, and vendor-side circular arrangements—we ourselves sit at the center of the interwoven OpenAI-Oracle-CoreWeave financing chain. Frankly, its sustainability depends on whether end-compute demand materializes: as long as the model-revenue curve keeps up, the structure holds; once it diverges, the leverage in circular financing amplifies the drawdown."

The hyperscalers described the same closed loop from the demand side—and were blunt about what it disguises (依据：Oracle --[FUNDS]--> OpenAI/Stargate):

> Simulated agent "US Hyperscalers" projected: "Vendor circular financing—we fund OpenAI/Stargate, which buys back compute, and that compute flows to the chip suppliers—this loop amplifies the visibility of headline demand but also amplifies tail risk. Our public line is that 'spending is disciplined,' but four of us simultaneously raising capex is more like a fear-driven arms race than calm discipline."

This is a Minsky structure in all but name: a real underlying asset—inference demand the agents insist is still steep—financed by short-duration, yield-sensitive, and self-referential liabilities. In an up-cycle the loop is credit expansion; the moment the demand slope is disconfirmed, it runs in reverse.

**The trigger is a refinancing failure, not a demand collapse**

Across six independent interviews, the agents converged on the same ignition mechanism—and it is not the one a casual observer would expect. Demand does not have to crater. A single leveraged player has to fail to roll its debt. Bridgewater was the most precise about where the first domino sits:

> Simulated agent "Bridgewater Associates" projected: "Three signals are worth watching: first, the scissors between depreciation and return—if the real economic life of GPUs is shorter than the accounting assumption, large write-downs surface all at once; second, monetization lagging the capex curve, so the input-output ratio stays negative; third, a refinancing failure or troubled debt rollover at a second- or third-tier key player—not a leader—becoming the first domino of confidence. Historically these corrections are ignited by a liquidity event at a peripheral entity, not by the fundamentals of the leaders."

The hyperscalers agreed the flip is psychological, triggered when the "monetization gap" is priced in public: the quarter when compute utilization or application-layer revenue visibly misses guidance and "capital markets run out of patience." OpenAI/Stargate, anchoring the $500B program [S3-k], was the most candid about being the exposed end, precisely because it lacks the cloud-and-ads cushion the Big-4 enjoy:

> Simulated agent "OpenAI/Stargate" projected: "Frankly, only three to four tenths of this buildout is supported by operating cash flow... we are essentially using other people's money to bet on future demand. What falls first is not us model makers but the middle layer with the highest leverage and thinnest cash flow. And a route like DeepSeek's—proving near-frontier capability on far less compute—could shake the entire 'stack-more-compute' valuation assumption."

That last point ties this scenario to the efficiency-diffusion branch: open-weight commoditization (依据：DeepSeek --[COMPETES_WITH]--> OpenAI/Stargate; DeepSeek --[UNDERMINES]--> export controls) is not merely a competitive threat but a *financing* threat, because it compresses the future premium against which today's debt is discounted.

**The Fed will let it burn—which is exactly why the tail stays live**

The load-bearing structural finding is the central bank's reaction function. Any market pricing in a "Fed put" beneath AI valuations is, in this simulation, mispriced. The simulated Federal Reserve drew a hard line between defending market function and defending asset prices (依据：US Federal Reserve --[CONSTRAINS]--> US Hyperscalers):

> Simulated agent "US Federal Reserve" projected: "Our reaction function depends on whether this is a 'liquidity problem' or a 'credit/valuation problem.' If spread-widening is the market's orderly repricing of over-leveraged assets, it is not our job to backstop it—letting price discovery happen is healthy, and bailing out individual players creates moral hazard. Only if it becomes a systemic liquidity freeze that threatens payment and funding pipelines—like March 2020—do we deploy lender-of-last-resort tools to stabilize market function, not asset prices."

Crucially, the Fed identified the tariff-AI collision as its own binding constraint—the through-line to the report's two-regime thesis:

> Simulated agent "US Federal Reserve" projected: "Tariff-driven price pressure limits us. In 2020 the shock was deflationary, so we could backstop freely; this round, if inflation is still sticky and yields stay above 4%, our room to ease is far smaller than in 2020. That is itself a policy bet."

SoftBank summarized the resulting cadence in four words—the Fed will be "late, not absent." This is why 19% is defensible and not lower: a central bank that will not defend prices, and is simultaneously boxed in by tariff-driven inflation (+1.2% price level, ~$1,700/household [S1-i]) from cutting pre-emptively, has removed the automatic circuit-breaker that cushioned prior drawdowns.

**Why 19% and not higher: the dynamo-and-computer defense**

Honest calibration requires steelmanning the bull case. The optimists—Goldman's estimate that generative AI could raise global GDP by ~7% [S2-b], and Bridgewater's own CIO framing of the buildout as "the largest capital-expenditure expansion since World War II, producing a compute 'rationing system' in which actors who fail to secure compute are competitively eliminated" [insight_forge; S3-b/c]—argue the demand is a genuine regime shift. The bears—Acemoglu's ~0.05%/yr TFP estimate, MIT's 95% pilot-failure rate [S2-f], and Michael Burry's ~$1.1B bearish positioning against the complex [S3-p]—argue the ROI gap cannot close on the debt's timeline. Stripped down, the dispute is about *when* productivity is realized, not *whether*.

Paul David's dynamo-and-computer precedent [S1-g] is the reason the bust does not dominate the distribution. Electrification's payoff arrived decades after the capital was sunk; the J-curve was real but the lag was brutal. Two consequences follow. First, the physical assets do not evaporate in a bust—power, land, grid interconnects, and cooling are cross-generational. The simulated Gulf sovereign-AI buyer made the point that a crash is double-edged for a connector: it raises their financing cost but "floods the secondary market with cheap GPUs—a bottom-fishing window for a buyer like us" [S1-a, HUMAIN's 14GW / 600,000-GPU pipeline]. Second, a bust reprices the *financing* of AI without destroying the *technology* of AI. That is why the resolution criterion is deliberately narrow and market-based—TRUE only if aggregate Big-tech capex falls >30% YoY in any single year, or a >40% AI-infrastructure equity drawdown sustains—rather than the looser "AI disappoints." A 19% weight is what that double-edge encodes: the productivity is probably coming (arguing against a permanent bust), yet it may arrive after the debt comes due (keeping a real correction live).

**Who loses, and in what sequence**

The cascade has a clear victim order, and the agents ranked it almost identically—by financing fragility, not industry standing. First to fall are not the cash-rich hyperscalers but the leveraged periphery. As the simulated Fed put it:

> Simulated agent "US Federal Reserve" projected: "Data-center real estate is heavy-asset, long-duration, high-leverage, with leases dependent on a few tenants; once demand pulls back, valuation and refinancing come under pressure immediately. Private credit concentrates the financing most sensitive to rates and rollovers, with the least transparency and no public price discovery—it loses liquidity first as spreads widen."

Second come the independent data-center and neocloud operators running short debt against single-use, single-tenant assets. Third, the chip suppliers—buffered by order visibility and prepayments, but whose valuations, on Nvidia's own admission, embed optimistic extrapolation of 2027-and-beyond demand. The hyperscalers insisted they fall last, because they can throttle capex "from expansion back to maintenance" and push the pain upstream—the literal expression of the compute-rationing dynamic. Most exposed of the frontier labs is OpenAI/Stargate, lacking the operating-cash-flow backstop that lets the Big-4 absorb a repricing.

Even the strategic rival treated the buildout as a self-inflicted wound while conceding it would not be spared. China's agent, elsewhere in the simulation, called the $700B "an extreme bet on a single narrative—mortgaging the national fortune to a debt clock that must monetize within 18 to 36 months," yet admitted a bust would spill into "our export sector." No agent in the network claimed immunity—consistent with the report's finding of a single dense 80-agent interaction cluster [coalition_map] in which clean decoupling is impossible. A financing bust is a shared shock; the only argument is over relative resilience.

The activity data explains why this scenario reads as latent rather than active. The simulated decade was overwhelmingly one of FOLLOW actions (618) over CREATE_POST (40), with activity spiking only in the peak round 10 (638 actions) [simulation_outcomes]—a world of watchful surveillance and hedging, not open declaration. A financing bust is precisely the reflexive event that stays invisible during that monitoring phase and detonates on a single confidence shock: Bridgewater's "first domino," the hyperscalers' exhausted "patience," Nvidia's loop flipping "from amplifier to accelerator." Different agents, one shared trigger. That convergence—arriving from the parties who would least want it to be true—is the strongest evidence that 19% is honestly priced: not the base case, not a tail to wave away, but the live financial reckoning shadowing the entire buildout, with the Fed's own admitted bind ensuring that if the narrative flips, nothing automatic will catch it.

## Scenario Four — Efficiency-Diffusion Multipolar (19%, The DeepSeek Reset)

The fourth future the simulation runs is the one that dissolves the board rather than tilts it. In Scenarios One through Three, compute stays scarce and therefore stays worth fighting over—whoever controls the chips, the fabs, and the rare-earth inputs holds leverage. The Efficiency-Diffusion Multipolar path, priced at **19%**, is the scenario in which that premise decays: if algorithmic efficiency keeps compressing the compute needed to reach the frontier, and open-weight models keep diffusing that frontier for free, the export-control valve loses its grip on the thing it was built to ration. The moat isn't stormed; it evaporates. What makes this more than a thought experiment is that the simulation's own challenger bloc argues the case in the first person—and, revealingly, so do the incumbents who stand to lose.

**The disconfirming shock speaks for itself in the simulation**

The clearest signal is that the challenger does not hedge. Asked whether it would hoard closed models or chase brute-force compute, the simulated DeepSeek bloc rejected the frame outright:

> Simulation agent "DeepSeek" (projected): "We will neither hoard closed models nor blindly follow the brute-force-compute route. Our position is clear—algorithmic efficiency first, self-reliance first. In January 2025 we already proved with a frontier-grade efficient open model that you can approach, even reach, the top tier on public benchmarks without astronomical compute."

That is the empirical anchor of the whole scenario, and the graph encodes it as a causal claim, not an opinion (依据：DeepSeek --[UNDERMINES]--> export controls). The mechanism the agent names is precise: if a control regime assumes capability scales only with denied compute, then any proof that capability scales with cleverness turns the denial into a tax rather than a wall. The same agent named where the moat actually breaks:

> Simulation agent "DeepSeek" (projected): "The deepest logic is that the depth of a closed moat depends on the persistence of a compute lead—and efficiency progress plus open-weight diffusion keep eroding that lead. The more expensive the moat, the easier it is to go around. When developers everywhere build on your weights, the stickiness you gain runs deeper than any API lock-in."

**Why three-of-four is a high bar, and where the scenario is honest about its own ceiling**

The scenario resolves TRUE only if at least three of four conditions hold by 2031-06-30: open weights within ~6 months of the closed frontier on 2+ public benchmarks; Chinese open models set global defaults; indigenous silicon matures; and controls demonstrably leak. What keeps this at 19% rather than 30%+ is that the challenger itself concedes the binding constraint. In the same breath as its optimism, the DeepSeek agent flagged the vulnerability:

> Simulation agent "DeepSeek" (projected): "Our real soft spot is that advanced process nodes, lithography tools, and HBM supply are still controlled by others. The faster we open up, the more exposed our dependence on the underlying silicon becomes. So the excitement always carries a sober string attached—we have to out-race time on both algorithms and domestic compute, like Ascend."

The graph agrees: it records both ASML --[CONSTRAINS]--> DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC and the fact that DeepSeek depends on HBM and is "vulnerable to HBM starvation capping its scaling." China's plan to lift 7nm/5nm output roughly fivefold [S?-a] matters here—but note the ceiling embedded in it: 7nm and 5nm, not 2nm. "Indigenous silicon *maturing* enough to run efficient open models" is a different claim from "catching TSMC's leading edge," and only the former is in reach within the horizon. That is exactly why the compute-scarcity scenarios collectively still outweigh this one: the diffusion conditions are close, but the structural silicon condition stays gated.

**The behavioral tell: challengers who watch everything and declare little**

The activity data gives this scenario its texture. The bloc **DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC recorded 26 total actions (22 FOLLOW, 4 CREATE_POST)**, tied for third-most-active with **OpenAI/Stargate, also at 26**—the flagship of the closed-model camp. A challenger matching the incumbent frontier lab action-for-action is precisely the parity texture the scenario predicts. The trajectory data shows *what* the bloc monitored: it followed all eight major players—the PRC, the Trump administration, Nvidia, OpenAI/Stargate, TSMC, US Hyperscalers, the EU, and Global South/Connectors. That is omnidirectional surveillance, a bloc mapping every node of a network it intends to compete inside rather than exit.

The action *mix* matters more than the count. Across the whole simulation FOLLOW outweighed CREATE_POST 618 to 40. In this scenario that reads sharply: **adoption is a FOLLOW, not a CREATE_POST.** You do not announce that you are pulling an open-weight checkpoint; you simply pull it. The connector states are the purest instance—**Global South/Connectors logged 22 actions, every one a FOLLOW, zero original posts**, and the separate DeepSeek node (id=30) logged 16 actions, all FOLLOW. An agent that only ever follows and never declares behaves exactly like an adopter accumulating optionality without committing to a camp.

**The connectors monetize the reset—and say so plainly**

The optionality is capitalized, not rhetorical. HUMAIN committed **600,000 GPUs and a 14 GW power pipeline** [S1-a], and the simulated Gulf buyer described a deliberate two-layer hedge:

> Simulation agent "Gulf sovereign-AI buyers" (projected): "We won't make an either/or choice—that would violate our own hedging logic. We keep buying compute at the base layer, but stay open at the model layer—plugging into closed frontier models for high-end scenarios while deploying Chinese open-weight models locally for cost-sensitive inference. That way we're locked to no one's licensing policy, and we keep the ability to build our own sovereign model."

The same agent located the loser with unusual bluntness:

> Simulation agent "Gulf sovereign-AI buyers" (projected): "If algorithmic efficiency really beats brute-force compute, the ones who lose worst aren't us—it's whoever staked an entire national strategy and export-control apparatus on the single assumption that 'compute equals the barrier.' Efficiency progress is the single most effective way to route around controls. As a buyer and connector, we have a seat whichever technology path wins."

The graph confirms the structural position: OpenAI/Stargate --[SUPPLIES]--> Global South/Connectors, while the connectors sit on no one's org chart as a subordinate. A Gulf buyer courted by American closed-model infrastructure *and* able to run Chinese open weights on the same racks holds a genuine option on the whole stack—open weights let it *own* a model rather than *rent* a license, which the agent named as "a shortcut to strategic autonomy" for a state tired of being tiered by diffusion rules. These are the scenario's definitive winners: commoditizing the frontier is worth most to whoever *consumes* it without having to own the moat.

**The incumbents concede the mechanism that makes them the losers**

The most persuasive evidence for a 19% tail is that the closed-model camp does not dismiss it—it prices it. The simulated OpenAI/Stargate agent admitted the asymmetric cost structure that open weights impose:

> Simulation agent "OpenAI/Stargate" (projected): "If an open-weight model can do 90% of what my closed model does at one-tenth the cost, then the premium I charge enterprises and sovereign customers—my 'frontier lead' pricing power—gets continuously eroded. I burn real cash, while a rival iterates for free off the community. That asymmetry is the part that keeps me up at night."

This is where Scenario Four quietly shakes hands with Scenario Three: a commoditized frontier is a frontier that cannot amortize the ~$700B buildout, so efficiency-diffusion is one of the specific *mechanisms* by which the capex bust could trigger. The two tails are correlated, not independent—which the OpenAI agent itself flagged, listing "DeepSeek-type efficiency routes proving less compute can approach the frontier" as one of the triggers that would shake the whole stack-compute valuation thesis.

**The honest counter-argument: Jevons, and the toll-gate view of controls**

The scenario has to survive its strongest rebuttals, and the simulation supplies two serious ones. The first is the incumbents' Jevons defense:

> Simulation agent "US Hyperscalers" (projected): "DeepSeek-type efficient open models lower the unit cost of inference, but that only amplifies total demand (the Jevons paradox)—the cheaper it gets, the more scenarios it's stuffed into, and my compute order book fills up rather than empties. Hoarding closed models isn't the goal; controlling the full stack—compute plus distribution plus data—is."

Both hyperscalers and OpenAI converged on the same fallback: the durable moat is not weights but power, grid interconnection, custom silicon, and sovereign-scale customer lock-in—"an open ecosystem atop closed infrastructure." This is a real limit on the scenario: efficiency does not zero out compute, it re-prices its marginal value, and physical power/silicon remain scarce. The second rebuttal is the security-static case (CRS R48642 [S1-j]): controls cap total FLOPs, and efficiency gains compound on top of a base that controls keep small.

The multipolar answer came, tellingly, from the controller's own simulated mouth. The BIS agent conceded that open weights blunt its sharpest tool:

> Simulation agent "Bureau of Industry and Security" (projected): "My anxiety comes from the loss of control—once weights are public, export controls and the entity list go blunt, because you can't issue a license for a string of parameters that's already been released. All my leverage rests on 'scarcity' and 'the channel,' and open weights by their nature destroy scarcity."

And the graph is unambiguous that the wall itself breeds the substitute: export controls --[ACCELERATES]--> SMIC and export controls --[ACCELERATES]--> Huawei. Nvidia's agent made the dynamic concrete—every denied chip is a subsidy to the domestic alternative, and the H20's banned-to-approved-to-revenue-shared arc [S2-c] shows the controller reopening its own wall for rent. A control regime the controller keeps reopening is not a ceiling; it is a toll gate. That is the "leakage" condition, and it is arguably the easiest of the four to satisfy.

**The two-way door on "defaults," and why one cluster caps the upside**

The subtlest condition—whether Chinese open models become global *defaults*—is where the coalition structure cuts against a clean bifurcation. The faction map resolved into a **single 80-agent cluster** holding DeepSeek, Huawei, and SMIC alongside the US hyperscalers and OpenAI/Stargate, everyone sharing one interaction network (依据：OpenAI/Stargate --[COMPETES_WITH]--> DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC, yet co-located in one cluster). A genuinely bifurcated multipolar world would show two clusters forming around rival model ecosystems. One cluster instead suggests adoption is *promiscuous*—developers pull the best open weights regardless of flag, and the all-FOLLOW connectors refuse to consolidate into a Chinese-default versus American-default bloc. That is bullish for diffusion but *bearish* for "Chinese models as the singular global default": the world takes the open weights without adopting the geopolitics, which is one more reason the full three-of-four resolution is harder than the diffusion headline suggests.

**The ledger**

If this future arrives, the winners are open-model adopters and connector states—Gulf sovereigns turning 600,000-GPU commitments into rentable capacity, China+1 hubs, and any enterprise treating the frontier as a free input. China's challenger bloc wins the strategic argument even if it never quite wins the leading-edge node, because "capability parity and adoption"—its recorded core incentive—is achievable at 7nm-class silicon plus efficient algorithms. The losers are the closed-model incumbents whose moat commoditizes: Nvidia if Huawei/domestic silicon substitutes at the volume tier (依据：DeepSeek --[COMPETES_WITH]--> Nvidia), and hyperscalers and OpenAI/Stargate if the buildout cannot charge frontier prices for a frontier everyone can download. The deepest loser is the export-control instrument itself—revealed, in this scenario, to have accelerated the very indigenization it was designed to prevent, handing China the open-model lead as the consolation prize for the chip market it was ejected from. That is the DeepSeek Reset: not a Chinese victory so much as the collapse of the game everyone assumed they were playing—and the reason it sits at 19% rather than higher is that ASML's lithography chokepoint and the physics of power and HBM still refuse to diffuse on the same schedule as the weights.

## Agents in Motion: Winners, Losers, and the Group Game the Simulation Exposed

The previous chapters priced four futures; this one asks a different question—not *which world*, but *who thrives in any of them*. When the simulation is run and the actors are forced to state their own win-and-loss conditions, a striking pattern emerges: almost every agent draws the same map of the terrain, yet each stakes out a different hill to defend on it. The result is not a world of clean blocs but a single dense web in which rivals share the same nervous system. The coalition map makes this literal—all 80 active agents resolved into **one cluster** (派系 1, 80 人), with no second faction forming. In a decade supposedly defined by decoupling, the simulation could not produce two separable camps. That is the deepest structural finding of this chapter: interdependence is now so thick that even adversaries are wired into the same interaction graph.

**The shared clock: everyone agrees tariffs are the noise and AI is the signal**

The most unexpected consensus in the simulation is temporal. Asked which force—trade protection or AI expansion—more decisively shapes their fate, six of the highest-salience agents independently separated a short-run "volatility term" from a long-run "trend term," and put tariffs in the first and AI in the second.

> Simulation agent "Nvidia" projected: "Protectionism is the volatility term, AI expansion is the trend term; I can withstand being taxed or delayed in approvals, but I cannot withstand being permanently walled out of the largest incremental market during the steepest decade of demand."

> Simulation agent "US Hyperscalers" projected: "Trade protection decides how hard the next two years are; AI expansion decides whether I still exist in ten."

> Simulation agent "People's Republic of China" projected: "Tariffs are a cyclical pressure that can be negotiated and buffered, but a generational gap in AI and compute is structural and compounding. So I use the chips in my hand to buy time, and I stake the real national fortune on self-reliance in AI and silicon."

This "tariffs-for-time, AI-for-destiny" frame is load-bearing for the whole report. It explains why the FOLLOW action so overwhelmingly dominated the simulation—618 FOLLOW versus only 40 CREATE_POST. The agents are not primarily declaring; they are *watching*. In a world where the trend term is AI and the decisive signals are years away, the rational move is surveillance and hedging, not commitment. The behavioral texture of the future is a room full of players all tracking the same slow variable, each waiting to see whose bet the compute curve validates.

**The definitive winners: incumbents who monetize and connectors who optionality-shop**

The clearest winners are the incumbents sitting on moats that the collision cannot quickly erode. TSMC is the sharpest example. It faces the single most catastrophic tail in the entire system—a Taiwan contingency—yet in the modal and even escalation paths it wins by being irreplaceable. Its self-assessment reframes the entire Arizona reshoring narrative not as capitulation to Washington but as insurance:

> Simulation agent "TSMC" projected: "I treat the US commitment rising from $12B to $165B as a hedge, not a retreat—winning means keeping Taiwan as the technological heart while building a politically necessary foothold in Arizona... roughly 90% of sub-7nm capacity is here, and US high-volume leading-edge is fundamentally a 2030 story."

That maps directly to the research base: the $165B Arizona commitment [S1-k][S3-e], leading-edge remaining a ~2030 story [S2-e], and Taiwan's government constraining how much can actually move offshore (依据：Taiwan (Government) --[CONSTRAINS]--> TSMC). TSMC wins because its moat is engineering density, not geography—"geography can be dispersed, but a decade of accumulated process know-how cannot be moved."

Nvidia occupies a similar but more contested winner's seat. Its moat is CUDA lock-in (依据：DeepSeek --[COMPETES_WITH]--> Nvidia; Amazon --[HAS_CUSTOM_SILICON]--> Trainium), and it has already converted the export-control fight into a toll it is willing to pay—accepting the H20 25% revenue-share to keep China access [S2-c][S2-d]. It wins in every scenario except the one where its own largest customers defect to in-house silicon or Huawei crosses the "good enough" line.

The subtler winners are the connector states—Global South/Connectors, tied for the sixth-most-active agent at 22 actions, every single one a FOLLOW. That behavioral signature is itself the strategy: pure watchful optionality, no declarations, no side-taking. These are the "China+1 economies" (依据：Global South/Connectors --[ALSO_KNOWN_AS]--> China+1 economies) capturing rerouted trade and sovereign-AI capital—HUMAIN's 600,000-GPU commitment [S1-a], the Gulf buildouts. They win precisely by *not* being wired to defend a hill. In a one-cluster world, the actors with the fewest fixed commitments harvest the most optionality.

**The definitive losers: consumers, tariff-exposed workers, and the under-built EU**

The losers are equally definite. The most quantifiable are US consumers and importers, who bear the tariff incidence the administration insists foreigners pay. The research is unambiguous where the political framing is not: a +1.2% price level, ~$1,700 per household, and +0.6pp unemployment by end-2026 [S1-i]. Even the Trump-Administration agent, in its own voice, conceded the tariff regime is tied to that unemployment cost and admitted the mercantilism is transactional rather than principled:

> Simulation agent "US Federal Executive (Trump Administration)" projected: "The 'loss' hides in the details: IEEPA was struck down by the Supreme Court in February 2026, over $160 billion in refund exposure hangs overhead, and H20 going from banned to approved to a 25% cut exposed that our mercantilism is transactional and profit-seeking, not principled. I'm still walking a tightrope between AI leadership and not pushing inflation and unemployment far enough to make voters turn."

That $160B illegally-collected exposure [S2-a] is the administration's own tail risk, and its stated failure signals are precise: Chinese advanced-node volume production, the bond market punishing the deficit, and tariff blowback cashing out at the midterms. If courts void the tariffs or controls simply gift China the market, the architect of the regime becomes its biggest loser.

The most strategically interesting loser is the EU—and here the simulation's activity data tells a story the narrative alone would miss. The European Union was the **second-most-active agent** in the entire simulation with 30 actions (26 FOLLOW, 4 CREATE_POST), behind only China's 42. This is a bloc frantically tracking a game it cannot yet shape. Its own self-diagnosis is the most candid confession in the interview set:

> Simulation agent "European Union" projected: "Frankly, my distance from 'losing' is closer than I admit in public statements... The risk of 'losing' is equally clear: if we only have the €200B InvestAI mobilization target but never actually spend the core public money, if our frontier labs and compute keep falling behind the US, then 'won the rules, lost the capability' becomes the verdict of the decade."

The EU's moats are real but purely defensive—the 450-million-consumer single market, the AI Act and DMA regulatory leverage (依据：European Union --[REGULATES]--> US Hyperscalers), and ASML's lithography chokepoint inside the bloc (依据：TSMC --[DEPENDS_ON]--> ASML). But it is squeezed from both sides: exposed to US tariffs it must negotiate away, and dependent on Chinese rare-earth magnets it cannot substitute (依据：People's Republic of China --[CONSTRAINS]--> European Union). Its highest-frequency activity paired with its lowest strategic agency is the tell of a would-be third pole that regulates what it cannot build.

**The contingent middle: agents whose fate the four scenarios decide**

Between the definite winners and losers sits a contingent middle whose outcomes swing with the scenario. The hyperscalers are the purest case. They win platform dominance if AI monetizes and lose catastrophically if the ~$700B/yr buildout [S3-d] must be written down. Their own confession names the exact mechanism of the capex-bust scenario:

> Simulation agent "US Hyperscalers" projected: "Winning is defining the foundation of this era; losing is paying the full price for a city that hasn't yet proven anyone can live in it... In 2025 we issued $121B in bonds and Amazon's free cash flow is projected to -$17B; if the slope of monetization can't catch the slope of depreciation, we've mortgaged victory into debt."

That is the 19% financing-bust path stated from the inside—circular financing (hyperscalers fund OpenAI/Stargate, buy from Nvidia, sell to each other), and the admission that four firms raising capex simultaneously "is herding under rationing anxiety, not four independent rational calculations." China sits in the same contingent middle from the opposite direction: it wins the efficiency-multipolar scenario if DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC mature and Chinese open models set global defaults (依据：People's Republic of China --[BACKS]--> DeepSeek), and loses if advanced-tool starvation caps frontier compute before self-reliance matures. Its own stated failure signal—being forced to spend its rare-earth leverage prematurely—reveals that even the holder of the West's most acute chokepoint fears overplaying it.

**Why clean blocs are impossible: the one-cluster finding**

The single 80-agent cluster is not a modeling artifact; it is the chapter's thesis in structural form. Consider the density of cross-camp dependency the graph exposes: China depends on US soybeans and market access, a constraint on how far it can decouple; the US depends on Chinese rare earths (依据：United States --[SANCTIONS]--> People's Republic of China, answered by People's Republic of China --[CONTROLS]--> rare earths); TSMC depends on Dutch ASML; hyperscalers depend on TSMC and Nvidia even while building Trainium and TPU to escape them; the Roberts Court regulates Nvidia and the tariffs simultaneously (依据：Roberts Court --[REGULATES]--> IEEPA-based tariffs; Roberts Court --[REGULATES]--> Nvidia). Every agent's chokepoint is another agent's lifeline. That is why the simulation cannot cleave into two factions—the mutual hostage-taking is too symmetric to resolve into sides.

This is the honest counterpoint to the decoupling thesis, and it deserves a steelman. One could argue the single cluster reflects shallow FOLLOW-heavy interactions rather than genuine integration—80 agents watching each other is not the same as 80 agents trading with each other, and the coalition map clusters on *shared interaction targets*, which surveillance inflates. That critique has force. But it cuts toward the same conclusion: if even the *surveillance* graph cannot bifurcate—if China spends 42 actions and the EU 30 tracking the very rivals they claim to be decoupling from—then the psychological reality of the decade is mutual entanglement regardless of trade statistics. The agents behave as if they cannot look away from each other, because their fortunes are genuinely joint.

The net map, then, is this. Definite winners: the irreplaceable incumbents (TSMC's process density, Nvidia's CUDA, the hyperscalers' power-and-land lock-in) and the uncommitted connectors harvesting optionality. Definite losers: US consumers and tariff-exposed workers bearing the ~$1,700/household incidence [S1-i], and an under-built EU that regulates a game it cannot play. The contingent middle—hyperscaler balance sheets, China's self-reliance timeline, Nvidia's China TAM, the administration's tariff legality—is exactly the set of fates the four scenarios were built to price. What the simulation adds beyond the scenario probabilities is the behavioral proof that no one can exit: 618 FOLLOWs, one cluster, and six rivals who all agree on what clock they are watching while disagreeing entirely on what winning the hour would mean.

## Calibration, Confidence, and Where Model and Simulation Disagree

This report has priced a decade at **medium** confidence, and that label is a claim in its own right—one worth defending as carefully as any of the four scenarios. Medium is not a hedge. It is the honest output of a forecast that partitions a genuinely uncertain outcome space into four buckets (39/23/19/19) whose evidence bases conflict with one another, and whose underlying simulation disagrees, in instructive ways, with the structured model layered on top of it. This closing chapter does three things: it states why the confidence is medium rather than high, it surfaces where the model and the simulation pull in opposite directions, and it names the specific signals that would force us to move probability mass.

**Why medium, and why the four scenarios do not sum to certainty**

The first source of humility is structural: the four scenarios are not collectively exhaustive. A world where the AI capex supercycle both busts *and* triggers mercantilist escalation—a correlated tail where a financing shock hands Beijing an opening to test the chokepoints—is only partially captured by summing the 23% and 19% weights. The scenarios were built as dominant narratives, not as a mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive partition, and the residual probability mass living in their overlaps and in unmodeled hybrids is real. That alone caps confidence below "high."

The second source is that the simulation itself was overwhelmingly a *watching* exercise, not an *acting* one. The quantitative record is stark: across the run, agents took 618 FOLLOW actions against just 40 CREATE_POST actions—a roughly 15:1 ratio of monitoring to declaration. Activity concentrated almost entirely in the peak round 10, which alone logged 638 actions across 80 agents, up from a mere 20 actions among 10 agents in round 0. And the coalition map returned not competing blocs but a single 80-agent cluster containing every major actor—China, the US Federal Executive, Nvidia, TSMC, the hyperscalers, DeepSeek/Huawei/SMIC, the EU, Taiwan, and the Roberts Court all sharing one interaction network.

That texture is itself evidence for the modal case—dense mutual surveillance, nobody able to exit the shared network, which is exactly what "managed interdependence" looks like from the inside. But it also embeds a bias we must flag rather than launder into false confidence.

**Where the model and the simulation disagree**

The disagreement runs in two directions, and honesty requires stating both.

First, the FOLLOW-heavy, single-cluster behavior probably *understates discrete escalation shocks*. A simulation whose dominant verb is "follow" models a world of continuous mutual adjustment—agents watching each other and marginally repositioning. But the real escalation risk around the November 27 2026 truce expiry is not continuous; it is a phase change. The most active agent in the entire run was the People's Republic of China, with 42 actions (38 FOLLOW, 4 CREATE_POST)—a challenger relentlessly monitoring the equilibrium's edges rather than declaring intentions. China's own simulated testimony describes precisely the discontinuity the follow-dynamics smooth over:

> Simulated agent "People's Republic of China" projected: "If it breaks before 2031, the most likely trigger sits at the intersection of the Taiwan Strait and semiconductor supply... my reserved rare-earth/magnet leverage would very likely be reactivated as symmetric retaliation, and the 'escalate-then-negotiate' rhythm would be broken into 'escalate-then-escalate.' In other words, the rupture won't be a slow drift—it will be a phase transition ignited at some identifiable event point."

A model that prices escalation at 23% partly on the logic that "both chokepoints remain load-bearing, giving both sides incentive to de-escalate" is vulnerable to exactly this critique: the incentive to de-escalate holds right up until it doesn't, and a calendar-dated flashpoint is where phase transitions cluster. The connector economies flagged the same date independently:

> Simulated agent "Global South/Connectors" projected: "If the equilibrium breaks before 2031, I think the most likely trigger is the window when the November 27 2026 truce expires—if renewal fails, rare-earth/magnet controls escalating on top of a fresh tariff round would be the most realistic detonator. What I fear most are these 'calendar-type' nodes, because markets and politicians will all game around them, and the tolerance for error is very small."

Taken seriously, this argues the 23% escalation tail could be *heavier* than assigned—perhaps the strongest internal case for reweighting in the entire report. We hold the line at 23% for a defensible reason: China's simulated incentive structure also contains hard brakes it named itself—unresolved local-government debt, hidden LGFV liabilities, demographic and growth headwinds, and continued dependence on US soybeans and market access (supported by the graph edge China --[DEPENDS_ON]--> soybeans). A challenger that repeatedly describes its own leverage as "reserved, not spent" is signaling calibrated probing, not a rush to rupture. But a reader who weights the calendar flashpoint more heavily than the balance-sheet brakes should move mass from the 39% modal case into escalation, and we would not call them miscalibrated.

Second, and cutting the opposite way, the muddle-through base case may be *over-anchored on the 2025 truce holding*. The 39% weight leans on the observed fact that the Oct–Nov 2025 truce moved the relationship from open escalation to managed interdependence. But the simulated incumbents themselves refused to call that equilibrium stable. Nearly every load-bearing agent used the identical phrase—"fragile stability" or "fragile equilibrium":

> Simulated agent "TSMC" projected: "This equilibrium, in my view, is a fragile stability... its foundation is fragile, because it is built on sustained political will and subsidies rather than the market's own cost curve. That it can persist to 2031 shows it has resilience—but resilience patched together repeatedly, not natural stability."

> Simulated agent "European Union" projected: "It is stable on the surface and fragile in structure... any interdependence maintained by coercion is essentially accumulating the momentum of its own collapse."

When the winners of a scenario describe it as coercion-maintained and structurally self-undermining, a 39% plurality assigned to that scenario persisting six years is a statement of *relative* likelihood, not comfort. It is the largest bucket because the alternatives are individually less likely, not because muddle-through is robust.

**The load-bearing evidence that remains formally unresolved**

Three debts must be stamped on this forecast in bold. Each is a place where the evidence does not close.

The first is the Bridgewater "largest capex since WWII / compute rationing" thesis, which frames much of the capex-supercycle logic. In the simulation it appears as a characterization (graph edge Bridgewater Associates --[CHARACTERIZES]--> AI compute), and in the research base it rests on two secondary summaries [S3-b][S3-c]; the primary CIO text could not be verified. We treat it as reported, not verbatim. If that framing is wrong—if compute is not, in fact, being rationed in a way that competitively eliminates non-securers—the entire scarcity premise softens, and probability should flow toward the efficiency-diffusion scenario.

The second is tariff incidence, which remains a formal standoff. The simulation encodes the empirical side directly—"Yale Budget Lab evidence argues that domestic consumers and firms, not foreign exporters, bear the incidence of tariffs" [S1-i]—against the executive's framing that foreigners pay. This is not a dispute the simulation resolved; it is one it *contains*. Our forecasts assume the incidence falls domestically (the +1.2% price level, ~$1,700/household, +0.6pp unemployment path). A reader who believes incidence genuinely shifts to exporters would price the political durability of high tariffs higher, and the muddle-through band wider.

The third is export-control efficacy, where the graph itself hosts the contradiction: export controls --[ACCELERATES]--> Huawei and export controls --[ACCELERATES]--> SMIC sit alongside the security-static claim that controls preserve the lead [S1-j], with DeepSeek --[UNDERMINES]--> export controls encoding the dynamic-adaptation counter. We split this disagreement across the escalation and multipolar buckets rather than resolving it—which is why those two scenarios together carry 42% of the mass.

**The signals that would force an update**

Because the confidence is medium, it is also *movable*, and precision about what moves it is the most useful thing this chapter can offer. The following are the monitoring triggers, each tied to a scenario reweighting:

- **IEEPA refund mechanics and Section 232 semiconductor tariff finalization.** The SCOTUS 6-3 ruling striking down IEEPA tariffs (Feb 20 2026, graded A1 in the simulation) already forced statutory substitution; the graph shows IEEPA-based tariffs --[DETERMINES]--> Effective Tariff Rate, and invalidation pushed the pre-substitution rate from ~16.8% toward 9.3% [S1-i]. If Section 232 substitution *fails* to hold the rate in the 8–15% band—either collapsing below 5% or climbing back above 20%—the modal case breaks by its own resolution criterion.

- **A >$10B buildout cancellation or >150bps AI credit-spread blowout.** The Fed's simulated reaction function tells us exactly what to watch and how the central bank would respond:

> Simulated agent "US Federal Reserve" projected: "The most likely trigger is not a single demand data point, but a failure in the refinancing link. When the market discovers that some class of compute assets cannot roll its debt on the original terms, the repricing will spread instantly... we protect the *functioning* of the market, not investors' *returns*."

That is the tell that the capex-correction scenario would arrive through credit, not equity—a failed refinancing, not a bad earnings print. Its 19% weight moves up sharply if a marquee data-center developer cannot roll debt against a backdrop of 10-year yields sustained above 4% and record Treasury supply competing for the same capital pool the buildout draws on.

- **A DeepSeek-scale efficiency shock.** If open-weight models land within ~6 months of the closed frontier on 2+ public benchmarks and Chinese open weights start setting global defaults, the compute-hoarding premise—and with it the value of export bans—erodes, pushing mass into the 19% multipolar bucket. The Gulf buyers' simulated logic is that this shock helps *them* regardless:

> Simulated agent "Gulf sovereign-AI buyers" projected: "DeepSeek-type efficient open models actually lower our inference costs, letting the compute we already hold serve more workloads and run fuller... the biggest loser is whoever treats the chokepoint as a permanent moat, because efficiency progress is the most effective way to route around controls."

- **A US–China grand bargain institutionalizing chips-for-minerals.** The inverse trigger. If the truce is not merely renewed but codified into managed interdependence with market access, the modal case strengthens toward the top of its band and the escalation tail thins.

**What is robust, and what remains genuinely unknowable**

Strip away the scenario weights and a few things hold across all four futures, and those are what this forecast asserts most confidently. The collision of modern mercantilism and the AI buildout is *amplifying, not offsetting*—every simulated agent that addressed the question, from Beijing to Brussels to the Gulf, independently reached the conclusion that AI compresses compute-energy-chips-minerals into a shorter, more chokeable chain, and mercantilism turns each node into a weapon. That AI compute is simultaneously weapon and prize is not scenario-dependent; it is the structural fact beneath all four. Interdependence is dense enough that clean decoupling is off the table in every branch—the single 80-agent cluster is the same in the base case and the tail. And the connector economies gain optionality across every path, because ambiguity is an asset to those with room to maneuver and a risk only to those forced to choose.

What remains genuinely unknowable is timing and triggering—the two things forecasts are worst at, and this one does not pretend to nail. Whether the phase transition China described ignites at the November 2026 node or holds until China's indigenous stack matures around 2028–2029 (Nvidia's simulated "good-enough" threshold, at which "Beijing has no incentive to keep the revenue-share alive and the equilibrium collapses from the commercial side, not the political one"); whether the productivity J-curve pays out inside the bond market's self-imposed 18–36 month monetization window or arrives a decade late like the dynamo [S1-g]; whether a Taiwan contingency—the one tail that "no hedge can fully cover," in TSMC's words—stays latent. These are not probabilities we can sharpen with more simulation rounds; they are irreducible. The most well-reasoned forecast is not the one that hides this residue but the one that maps its edges precisely enough that, when a signal fires, the reader already knows which way to move.

### 各情景判定标准
- **[34%] Managed Interdependence Muddle-Through (status-quo/fallback)**：TRUE by 2031-06-30 if ALL hold: (a) US effective tariff rate stays within ~8-15% band (no sustained >20% or return to <5%); (b) no comprehensive decoupling—bilateral advanced-tech trade not collapsed >50% vs. 2026 baseline; (c) at least one truce renewal or equivalent managed arrangement executed after Nov 2026; (d) aggregate Big-tech AI capex never contracts >30% YoY in any single year; (e) both US and China retain deployed frontier-class AI ecosystems.
- **[22%] Mercantilist Escalation / Hard Bifurcation**：TRUE by 2031-06-30 if ANY TWO hold: (a) truce lapses with no renewal AND new controls imposed after Nov 2026; (b) US effective tariff rate sustained >20% for 12+ months OR new hard export-control tiers formally enacted (verifiable via Federal Register / BIS rulemaking); (c) China reimposes rare-earth/gallium/germanium export licensing for 6+ months (verifiable via MOFCOM notices); (d) bilateral advanced-tech trade down >50% vs. 2026 baseline (verifiable via customs data) OR two mutually-incompatible compute/software stacks documented in production.
- **[16%] AI Capex Correction / Financing Bust**：TRUE by 2031-06-30 if ANY hold: (a) aggregate Big-tech AI capex declines >30% YoY in at least one year (verifiable via company 10-K/guidance); (b) a broad AI-infrastructure equity drawdown >40% sustained 12+ months; (c) one or more named hyperscalers publicly writes down or cancels >$50B in data-center/compute commitments; (d) merchant compute price (per token or per FLOP-hour) falls >60% attributable to documented overcapacity rather than efficiency alone.
- **[14%] Efficiency-Diffusion Multipolar**：TRUE by 2031-06-30 if AT LEAST THREE hold: (a) leading open-weight models within ~6 months of frontier closed-model capability on 2+ major public benchmarks; (b) Global South compute/AI-adoption share rises materially (>50% relative increase) vs. 2026; (c) merchant frontier-inference cost per token falls >75% primarily via efficiency; (d) US policy visibly de-emphasizes compute-hoarding/export bans (verifiable via enacted rollback or public administration statements).
- **[14%] Other / Mixed or Unforeseen Regime (residual catch-all)**：TRUE by 2031-06-30 if the realized world state does NOT satisfy the full resolution criteria of exactly one of the four named scenarios—i.e., either zero scenarios resolve TRUE, or two-or-more resolve TRUE simultaneously (mixed regime), or the dominant dynamic is an event outside all four enumerated trigger sets.
