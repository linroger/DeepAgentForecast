# The US Trading System in 2028: A Calibrated Forecast

**Forecast as of: July 3, 2026 | Horizon: December 31, 2028**

---

## Executive Summary

The US trading system stands at its most unstable constitutional moment since the Smoot-Hawley era. The February 20, 2026 Supreme Court ruling in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* invalidated the legal foundation of the Trump administration's signature reciprocal tariff regime, triggering a $166–175 billion refund obligation to roughly 330,000 importers across 53 million entries. The administration has responded with a three-pillar "Plan B" — Section 122 (15% global tariff, in litigation), Section 232 sectoral tariffs (now active on steel, aluminum, copper, semiconductors, and pharmaceuticals), and Section 301 investigations (60 countries targeted). The effective US tariff rate stands at approximately 8.95% (CBP customs revenue basis) to 10.6% (Yale Budget Lab import-weighted basis) as of January 2026.

The central thesis: The US trading system by 2028 will not be a coherent rules-based order. It will be a **fragmented, litigation-burdened patchwork of overlapping authorities**, with effective tariff rates between 7% and 12% depending on measurement methodology, elevated core PCE inflation 0.6–1.0 percentage points above the Fed's 2% target, a reshoring narrative significantly outpacing actual reshoring data, and a multilateral system from which the US is functionally absent but not formally withdrawn.

---

## Part I: Load-Bearing Thesis

**THESIS:** By December 31, 2028, the US will operate a *de facto* permanent tariff regime of 7–12% effective rate, sustained through a novel combination of Section 232 sectoral tariffs (covering ≥15% of import value) and renewed Section 122/301 actions, while the multilateral trading order continues its managed decline without US formal withdrawal — but this regime will impose a persistent inflation tax of approximately $2,000–2,800 per household annually, accelerate reshoring in semiconductors and pharmaceuticals while failing more broadly in consumer goods, and lock the US into transactional bilateralism with China, Mexico, and the EU that privileges supply-chain security over efficiency.

**Falsifiability conditions:** This thesis breaks if (a) the Federal Circuit upholds the CIT injunction on Section 122 tariffs and Congress fails to codify tariff authority before the 2026 midterms; (b) core PCE inflation falls below 2.5% by Q4 2027 (implying full demand absorption); (c) the Trump administration secures a comprehensive Section 232 codification with bipartisan congressional support; or (d) the US formally withdraws from the WTO.

## Part IV: Main Points of Contention and Flashpoints

### Contention 1: Tariff Authority Constitutional Crisis
**Core Dispute**: Whether IEEPA, Section 122, Section 232, and Section 301 provide adequate independent authority for the current tariff regime — or whether Congress must codify.

**Key Flashpoints**:
- Federal Circuit ruling on Section 122 merits (expected late 2026/early 2027)
- Congressional tariff codification attempts before 2026 midterms
- Potential Supreme Court review of Section 232 (untested post-*Learning Resources*)

### Contention 2: Inflation Passthrough Measurement
**Core Dispute**: Federal Reserve research (April 2026 FEDS Notes) finds +3.1% core goods PCE, +0.8% core PCE from tariffs — "full passthrough" to consumers. Fed Governor Stephen Miran dissents, arguing foreign firms absorbing cost.

**Quantitative Conflict**:
- Yale Budget Lab: $2,000–2,800 annual household burden
- Dallas Fed: Passthrough effects peaked February 2026 at "full passthrough" levels
- Miran: "Smaller than widely reported"

**Resolution Path**: Core PCE data through Q4 2026 will resolve empirically. Current data favors full-passthrough hypothesis.

### Contention 3: Reshoring Numbers Credibility
**Core Dispute**: Reshoring Initiative reports 250,000+ jobs in 2025; claims 1.4 million cumulative since 2010. Brookings and other academic sources question methodology.

**Key Metrics**:
- TSMC Arizona: First fab N4 high-volume production Q4 2024; second fab structure complete 2025, N3 production targeted H2 2027
- Samsung Taylor: Equipment move-in began April 24, 2026; production end-2026; qualified 2nm H2 2027
- Intel: Panther Lake Core Ultra Series 3 launched January 2026 (first 18A node client CPU); Fab 52 expansion continues
- Micron: $275M CHIPS funding for Manassas, Virginia modernization (proposed)

### Contention 4: USMCA Non-Renewal Implications
**Core Dispute**: Whether US non-renewal (July 1, 2026) triggers formal sunset or maintains operative status through annual reviews.

**Bipartisan Congressional Support**: House and Senate both show strong support for USMCA continuation despite Trump's posture; labor unions seek improvement but not termination.

**Negotiation Track**: Bilateral US-Mexico (Round 3: week of July 20), US-Canada (not yet started in earnest).

### Contention 5: China Rare Earth Leverage
**Core Dispute**: Whether Busan truce (one-year) is genuine detente or tactical pause.

**Key Data**: China's rare earth licensing to European buyers has fallen below 25% of prior levels; sixfold price spikes in tungsten/antimony (June 2026). Busan suspension runs until November 10, 2026.

**FORGE/Project Vault**: US and EU alternative supply chain programs — 60 Strategic Projects with 12–18 month window.

### Contention 6: Multilateral System Future
**Core Dispute**: Whether WTO reform is achievable or whether US-led fragmentation is terminal.

**MC14 Outcomes**: Fisheries subsidies negotiation continues to MC15; small economies package adopted; e-commerce moratorium unresolved; Yaoundé Package draft texts awaiting General Council.

**US Posture**: Greer absent from MC14; reform package blocking by US delegation; no formal withdrawal.

## Part VI: Cause-Effect Chains with Second-Order Effects

### Chain 1: SCOTUS Ruling → Section 122 → Section 232 Expansion → Sectoral Inflation
**First-order**: *Learning Resources* invalidates IEEPA tariff authority (Feb 20, 2026).
**Mechanism**: Administration pivots to Section 122 (15% global, 150-day window) and accelerates Section 232 sectoral actions (pharma April 2, semiconductors January, metals restructuring April 6).
**Second-order effect**: Sectoral tariffs concentrate inflation in specific CPI categories — pharmaceuticals (up to 100%), semiconductors (25% on high-end AI chips), metals (50% on steel/aluminum/copper). This creates CPI heterogeneity where headline may appear controlled while specific consumer categories experience sharp price increases.
**Third-order effect**: Pharmaceutical manufacturers face cost-pass-through decisions; semiconductor supply chain bifurcates (domestic AI chip production at TSMC Arizona vs. Nvidia H200 export controls); metals-intensive industries (autos, construction, appliances) experience margin compression.
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~75%. Evidence: Pharma Proclamation 11020 issued; Section 232 semiconductor tariffs in effect since January 2026; metals restructuring effective April 6, 2026.

### Chain 2: IEEPA Refund Pool → Fiscal Pressure → Bessent's "Snap Back" Strategy
**First-order**: $166–175B refund obligation created by SCOTUS ruling; $90B accepted, $24B transmitted to Treasury by June 26, 2026.
**Mechanism**: Treasury faces immediate budgetary hole; Bessent pledges tariffs will "snap back to exactly where they were" pre-SCOTUS via Section 232 + 122 + 301 layering.
**Second-order effect**: Congressional pressure to codify tariff authority increases — rank-and-file Republicans demand codification (per Axios February 20, 2026 reporting). This creates legislative vehicle for permanent tariff regime.
**Third-order effect**: Tariff revenue becomes structurally embedded in budget baseline; reducing tariffs becomes fiscally costly. This shifts the Overton window toward permanent tariffs as deficit-reduction tool.
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~65%. Evidence: Bessent's public statements; rank-and-file GOP pressure; CBO baseline assumptions incorporating tariff revenue.

### Chain 3: Busan Truce → Soybean Compliance → Rare Earth Tension → China Leverage Persistence
**First-order**: China commits to 12 mmt soybean purchases through August 2026; meets 11.96 mmt by end of May (per USDA FAS).
**Mechanism**: China suspends rare earth export controls until November 10, 2026; BIS suspends Affiliates Rule in exchange. Truce holds.
**Second-order effect**: China redirects rare earth pressure to Japan (licensing to EU falls below 25%); domestic chip production (Huawei, Cambricon) displaces Nvidia market share (collapsed from 95% to near-zero). Asia Times reports Nvidia's June 2026 Beijing visit failed to secure H200 sales.
**Third-order effect**: US semiconductor reshoring accelerates (TSMC Arizona N3 H2 2027, Samsung Taylor 2nm H2 2027) but loses Chinese end-market access permanently. FORGE/Project Vault launched but face 12–18 month supply chain window. Nvidia revenue base shifts further toward US hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon).
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~70%. Evidence: Busan truce holding; rare earth licensing data; reshoring milestones achieved; Nvidia China market collapse confirmed.

### Chain 4: USMCA Non-Renewal → Bilateral Pressure → Mexico Nearshoring Acceleration → Investment Concentration Risk
**First-order**: US declines to renew USMCA July 1, 2026; 10-year sunset review clock starts.
**Mechanism**: Mexico pursues USMCA modernization over renegotiation; Plan Mexico ($277B identified investment) positions Mexico as primary beneficiary; $40.87B FDI record in 2025.
**Second-order effect**: Investment concentration in northern Mexican manufacturing corridor creates infrastructure bottlenecks (energy, water, labor, security). Sheinbaum's 12% minimum wage hike (smaller adjustment in industrialized northern states) raises labor costs.
**Third-order effect**: Nearshoring narrative outpaces actual reshoring — Mexico's share of US imports grows while US manufacturing employment growth in reshored sectors remains modest (Reshoring Initiative 250K claims contested). Energy policy reversal + Pemex fiscal stress constrains industrial expansion.
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~80%. Evidence: Plan Mexico announced; FDI record; USMCA Joint Review proceeding bilaterally; energy constraints documented.

### Chain 5: CHIPS Act Production Era → AMIC Expiration → Investment Pause → 2027–2028 Capacity Gap
**First-order**: TSMC Arizona N4 high-volume production (Q4 2024); Samsung Taylor equipment move-in (April 2026); Intel Panther Lake 18A (January 2026).
**Mechanism**: AMIC (25% investment tax credit) expires December 31, 2026. SIA + 17 trade groups lobby for extension/expansion to 30–35%.
**Second-order effect**: Without AMIC extension, second-wave fab investment (TSMC Fab 2 N3, Samsung 2nm scale, Intel 14A) faces financing gap. Senate draft proposes 30%; SIA pushes 35%.
**Third-order effect**: If AMIC not extended by year-end 2026, 2027–2028 capacity additions slow materially; CHIPS Act achieves "production era" milestone but fails to achieve "expansion era." US semiconductor manufacturing share growth stalls at ~12–13% global capacity (vs. ~10% baseline).
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~50% for AMIC extension; ~50% for failure. Evidence: Bipartisan support; SIA lobbying; tariff revenue pressure; lame-duck session dynamics.

### Chain 6: Fed Warsh Confirmation → Hawkish Pivot → Growth-Inflation Tradeoff → Tariff Regime Lock-in
**First-order**: Warsh confirmed May 13, 2026 (54–45); first FOMC June 17 reveals hawkish dot plot (9 of 18 project hikes).
**Mechanism**: Warsh inherits tariff-driven inflation (+0.8% core PCE from tariffs alone); holds rates 3.50–3.75%; signals hikes possible.
**Second-order effect**: Higher-for-longer rate environment raises cost of reshoring capex; consumers face mortgage/rate headwinds on top of tariff inflation; small business investment declines.
**Third-order effect**: Trump pressures Warsh for cuts; Fed independence test mirrors 2019 Powell precedent but with stronger executive pressure. If Warsh capitulates, tariff-driven inflation becomes permanent; if he holds, growth slowdown creates political pressure for tariff reduction or codification.
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~60% for hawkish hold; ~40% for capitulation. Evidence: Warsh's hawkish credentials; SEP dot plot; Trump public pressure pattern.

### Chain 7: Section 301 Forced Labor Expansion → Supply Chain Compliance Costs → Multilateral Friction
**First-order**: USTR proposes 12.5% on 46 countries, 10% on 14 countries (June 2, 2026); comment deadline July 6; effective ~July 24.
**Mechanism**: Forced labor investigation touches virtually every major US trading partner; proposed tariffs cover ~$3–4T in import value.
**Second-order effect**: Compliance costs cascade through supply chains — Section 301 forced labor tariffs are universal in coverage; companies face documentation, auditing, and tariff exposure simultaneously.
**Third-order effect**: WTO dispute filings proliferate; EU and other partners file consultations; multilateral system faces legitimacy crisis as US uses trade remedy authority for non-trade objectives (labor standards enforcement through tariff mechanism).
**Probability of this chain materializing**: ~85%. Evidence: USTR announcement; comment process initiated; WTO already blocked on reform.

## Part IX: Non-Obvious / Contrarian Analysis

### What the Consensus Gets Wrong

**Consensus view 1**: "Trump's tariffs will be repealed or rolled back after SCOTUS ruling." This is wrong on two counts. First, the administration has already constructed a Plan B using Section 232/122/301 that does not depend on IEEPA. Second, Bessent has explicitly stated tariffs will "snap back to exactly where they were" via alternative authorities. The effective rate has *already* been restored through Section 232 metals restructuring (April 6, 2026) and pharmaceutical Proclamation 11020 (April 2, 2026). The consensus is anchored on IEEPA when it should be focused on the Section 232 architecture.

**Consensus view 2**: "Reshoring is succeeding." This is largely narrative rather than data. The Reshoring Initiative's 250,000 jobs figure for 2025 includes job announcements, not actual employment, and incorporates FDI that would have occurred regardless of tariffs. Semiconductor reshoring (TSMC, Samsung, Intel) is real and measurable, but represents a tiny fraction of US manufacturing employment. The 1.4 million cumulative figure since 2010 includes attribution effects that are difficult to verify. Brookings and academic critiques note that reshoring data systematically overstates by counting announcements and reclassifying existing facilities.

**Consensus view 3**: "USMCA will be terminated or fundamentally renegotiated." The July 1, 2026 non-renewal was politically dramatic but procedurally routine — it triggered a 10-year sunset review clock without terminating the agreement. Bipartisan congressional support for USMCA is strong; labor unions want improvement, not termination; business groups depend on stability. The agreement will likely be modified through bilateral negotiations but remain operational. The framing as "USMCA collapse" is media-driven, not policy-driven.

**Consensus view 4**: "China is winning the trade war." This is partially wrong. China's rare earth leverage is real and operational, but its semiconductor market access has collapsed (Nvidia from 95% to near-zero in China). The Busan truce reflects Chinese recognition that escalation costs exceed benefits, but also reflects US inability to decouple fully. Both sides are managing constraints rather than winning.

**Consensus view 5**: "The WTO is dead." The WTO is not dead; it is operating in a diminished capacity, much as GATT operated for 47 years before WTO formalization. MC14's deadlock was predictable given US posture; the Yaoundé Package's deferral to General Council is procedural, not terminal. The WTO's functions (dispute settlement for non-US parties, trade monitoring, technical assistance) continue. The relevant question is whether the WTO can produce new agreements, not whether it survives.

**Consensus view 6**: "Tariffs are fully passed through to consumers." The Yale Budget Lab and Federal Reserve research support this, but Fed Governor Stephen Miran argues the opposite — that foreign firms have absorbed significant cost. The Dallas Fed notes passthrough "peaked" in February 2026, suggesting some absorption has occurred. The aggregate data may overstate the incidence on US consumers if foreign exporters reduced margins to maintain market access. However, the core finding (tariffs raise consumer prices) remains valid; the dispute is about magnitude, not direction.

### The Structural Reality Behind the Noise

The US trading system in 2028 will be characterized by what we might call **"authority arbitrage"** — the exploitation of overlapping and partially redundant statutory authorities (Section 232, 301, 122, IEEPA before invalidation) to construct a tariff regime that is more resilient than any single statute would allow. The Section 232 metals restructuring (four-annex system with rates of 50%, 25%, 15% floor) is a technical masterpiece of legal architecture that survives challenges that would have felled the IEEPA reciprocal regime.

This regime will persist not because Congress willed it but because:
1. The administration has statutory tools independent of IEEPA
2. Section 232 has not been successfully challenged in court since 1980s
3. Section 301 trade-remedy authority has WTO cover
4. Tariff revenue reduces congressional appetite for repeal
5. Reshoring narrative creates political constituency for tariffs
6. Foreign retaliation is limited and selective

If the Federal Circuit reverses on the merits in late 2026 or early 2027 — or if the Supreme Court takes the case and rules broadly against executive tariff authority — the thesis breaks.

**What would have to be true for the thesis to break**:

2. Supreme Court extends *Learning Resources* logic to Section 232 or Section 301
3. Congress fails to enact any tariff codification before 2026 midterms
4. 2026 midterm election shifts House or Senate control to Democrats

## Part XVI: Concluding Assessment

The US trading system in 2028 will not be recognizable to observers from 2015. It will be a patchwork of overlapping authorities, sector-specific tariffs, bilateral managed relationships, and a weakened but operative multilateral framework. The effective tariff rate will remain elevated at 7–12%, sustained by Section 232 architecture that has proven more legally resilient than IEEPA.

The administration has constructed Plan B successfully — the *Learning Resources* ruling did not produce the tariff collapse that headlines suggested. Instead, Section 232 (metals, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals), Section 122 (temporary global), and Section 301 (forced labor) have collectively restored much of the pre-SCOTUS effective rate.

The costs are real and measurable: $2,000–2,800 annual household burden; +0.8% core PCE inflation contribution; fiscal pressure from $166–175B refund pool; and the structural risk that higher rates persist into the next administration regardless of political preferences.

The opportunities are concentrated: domestic semiconductor manufacturers, pharmaceutical reshoring, Mexico nearshoring, and defense/critical minerals supply chains benefit from the regime.

The multilateral system will persist in diminished form — WTO continues functioning without US leadership, much as GATT operated from 1947–1994. US-China relationship will be managed through bilateral channels with periodic escalation and de-escalation cycles. USMCA will survive non-renewal through continued bilateral negotiations.

The single most likely failure mode is constitutional fragmentation: a broad judicial ruling against executive tariff authority combined with congressional inability to codify. This would force rapid tariff reduction and a different regime. But this scenario carries only ~20% probability.

The most likely outcome — 55% probability — is fragmented continuity: a messy, legally questionable, but operationally stable tariff regime that persists into 2028 and beyond, imposing costs on consumers and importers while creating benefits for select domestic industries. This is not the trade policy the US designed, but it is the trade policy the US has.
